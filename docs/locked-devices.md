# Working while a device is locked

Status: research summary and proposed design rules, 2026-10-14. Nothing in this document has been tested on real hardware. Section 8 lists the tests, and Baton promises nothing that this page marks as unknown until they have been run.

Handing work off means walking away from the first device. By the time the second device needs something, the first is usually locked and often asleep. A hand-off tool that needs the first device to stay unlocked fails at its one job, so working while locked is a requirement for every milestone and not a feature of one (see the [roadmap](roadmap.md)).

This page records what each operating system allows, so that Baton promises only what it can keep, and the design rules that follow. It summarises three research passes: Windows; macOS, iOS and iPadOS; and Linux, Android and existing remote-access tools. The notes behind it are in [research/locked-devices/](research/locked-devices/README.md), so that the labels can be checked against their evidence. [architecture.md](architecture.md#locked-devices) keeps the short form of the rules.

## 1. The short answer

What Baton can plan to deliver:

- **Hand something off, then lock the device.** A link, text or file that the person sends while at the device is delivered when the other device can be reached, and a lock on the source does not stop that: a locked agent keeps delivering what the person explicitly sent (section 5.3). The "send and lock" gesture goes one step further. It waits for the other device to acknowledge the item and only then locks the source, so the item has left before the device can lock or sleep (section 5.6). Where the platform has a way to lock a device (Windows and Linux; Android only with privileged permissions; macOS has no documented one, and two public routes wait for test M12; iOS has none), Baton can lock the source for the person once the other device has acknowledged the item. The gap that remains is a source that goes to sleep before the other device appears. Only the mailbox of milestone 4 covers that. A phone as the source has less time: an iOS or iPadOS app has about five seconds after the lock to finish what the person sent (section 3), and an Android foreground service keeps delivering only while it stays alive (section 5.1).
- **A locked desktop that is still awake should keep receiving.** On Linux (reported), Windows (inferred, to be tested by W2) and macOS (inferred, with the qualifiers in section 3) a lock covers the screen but should leave the person's processes and network running, so the agent can accept items into its inbox, answer pings and deliver what the person sent. The person sees what arrived when they unlock.
- **Phones are reached through push notifications.** A locked iPhone or iPad suspends Baton within seconds, so a push notification is the only dependable route. A locked Android phone keeps a listener only through a foreground service, which the phone's maker may still stop.
- **Baton unlocks one thing only, when it is built.** Nothing unlocks yet; the design is [ADR 0013](adr/0013-unlock-from-a-phone.md). A Mac that the person set up at the Mac, for one paired phone or tablet, is to unlock when the person taps Unlock on that device and their face is recognised there, in a session that keeps the Mac dark and held. Nothing else is to unlock, not for another device and not as a convenience. See section 5.5.

What Baton cannot promise, because the platforms do not allow it:

- **Reading what is open on a locked device when asked.** The clipboard cannot be read in the background on iOS and Android, and GNOME offers no sanctioned way to do it. On Windows and macOS this is unknown, and macOS has announced an alert for programmatic pasteboard reads (section 3).
- **Showing or controlling the live windows of a locked device.** No documented API that Baton could use does this on any platform examined. The one product built around a locked device is Apple's iPhone Mirroring, which keeps the iPhone locked (documented), works only between an iPhone and a Mac, and cannot be reused. GNOME closes every remote-access session when the screen locks, Android stops screen capture on lock, and Windows refuses desktop capture on the secure desktop. Products that stream from a "locked" computer unlock it first and blank its screen, or start a separate session, or are given the password. Window streaming (milestone 5) has to be designed around this, and the first releases rely on locking the source after the hand-off.
- **Reaching a device that is asleep.** On desktops, sleep and not the lock is what stops the agent. On iOS and iPadOS the app is suspended shortly after the lock itself, and on Android Doze and the phone maker's background killers can end a foreground service (section 3). Waking a sleeping device takes Wake-on-LAN (best effort, hardware dependent), a push notification, or on the same network perhaps a sleep proxy (section 5.7).
- **Reaching a computer that has restarted and has not been signed in to**, or an iPhone that has not been unlocked since it booted.

## 2. How to read this page

Every claim carries one label:

- *documented*: stated in official documentation or a formal specification;
- *reported*: shown by source code, a maintainer's or vendor's statement, or a credible report. This is evidence, not a promise by the platform;
- *inferred*: reasoned from other facts. No source says it;
- *unknown*: no evidence either way. Each of these is on the list in section 8.

In the tables, **Works**, **Partly**, **No** and **Unknown** say what the evidence supports, followed by its label. Works (inferred) means "should work, and nobody has shown it". Nothing is Works (tested).

The states used:

- *Locked, awake*: the person has signed in, the lock screen is up and the machine has not gone to sleep. The screen may be on or off.
- *Asleep*: the machine went to sleep (power button, lid, idle timeout, menu).
- *Restarted*: the machine has booted and nobody has signed in yet. On a phone this means not unlocked since it booted.

How far the evidence goes:

- Nothing was run on hardware. Everything comes from documentation, source code and reports.
- Some pages reached the researchers only through a summarising tool, and some hosts were unreachable. Every Windows source was read that way, and so were the Apple forum, support and vendor pages, so the "documented" labels that rest on them rest on model-written summaries. Re-read these before the rules that depend on them are relied on: how the Desktop Activity Moderator times its suspension and throttles services, `LockWorkStation`, automatic sign-in after an update restart, and the five-minute limit on power requests (all Windows), and every statement about what a remote-access product does on a locked Mac. Apple's developer documentation and release notes were read as raw text, which covers the 30 seconds of `willSleep` and the launchd rule on quarantined property lists. The notes are in the repository and give the source of each claim. Facts added while the page was reviewed are in [review-additions.md](research/locked-devices/review-additions.md), which says how each page was read.
- The researchers ran out of search budget before they had covered everything. Section 9 lists the gaps.
- Operating systems change. The releases that matter are named where they do: Windows 11 24H2 changed how services are suspended, macOS 15 added Local Network privacy, Android 15 QPR1 stops screen capture on lock, and Android 17 adds a local-network permission for apps that target SDK 37.

## 4. On desktops, sleep is what stops the agent, not the lock

On phones it is different. An iOS or iPadOS app is suspended shortly after the lock itself, and Android's limits come from Doze and the phone makers' background killers (the last two rows).

| Platform | What stops it | What Baton can do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Windows | On Modern Standby, the Desktop Activity Moderator suspends the person's processes a few seconds after sleep starts. On S3 the whole system is suspended (documented). | A keep-awake request cannot stop a sleep that the person starts with the lid, the power button or the Start menu: all power requests end when a user-initiated sleep begins. On Modern Standby on battery, system-required and execution-required power requests end five minutes after the sleep timeout (both documented for `PowerSetRequest`). Whether `SetThreadExecutionState` behaves the same is not documented in the research, and W6 tests both. Staying awake during a hand-off is therefore possible only on AC power, and only until the lid closes (inferred). On Modern Standby a program must register for suspend notifications to receive the suspend message at all (reported). |
| macOS | System sleep stops the CPU (inferred). Power assertions are suggestions that lid close, low battery, thermal limits and Dark Wake override, and the user-idle assertions have no effect in Dark Wake (documented). | Hold an assertion only while a peer is connected, and expect a sleeping Mac to be unreachable. The will-sleep notification gives up to 30 seconds to finish what is running (documented). |
| Linux | Suspend freezes the person's processes (documented). The upstream defaults suspend an idle machine after 15 minutes on GNOME, and after 15 minutes on AC and 10 on battery on KDE (reported, from source code). What distributions set instead was not checked (unknown). In logind, closing the lid ignores ordinary `sleep` inhibitors by default and honours only the `handle-lid-switch` inhibitor (documented), but GNOME's and KDE's own lid handling differs from logind's: KDE's PowerDevil handles the lid itself, and what GNOME does with suspend inhibited is unknown (L1). | A delay inhibitor is allowed for every user (reported) and holds a suspend for five seconds at most by default (documented). Whether a user service may take a blocking inhibitor is unknown. Baton must not take an idle inhibitor just to stay reachable, because that also disables the person's auto-lock, and must not take the lid inhibitor by default, because a closed laptop in a bag would stay awake. |
| Android | Doze, app standby, phone makers' background killers and Low Power Standby. An automatic reboot after 72 hours locked is reported but not documented by Google. | A foreground service of a declared type, and the local-network permission. How much battery an always-on service costs is unknown. |
| iOS and iPadOS | The app is suspended shortly after the lock, and Wi-Fi may drop when the device sleeps. | Push notifications. There is no background agent. |

The rules that follow:

- **Assume every connection dies when the device sleeps.** After every resume the agent treats all peer connections as dead: it closes them, announces itself again on the local network and reconnects to the relay.
- **Hold a sleep inhibitor only while a transfer or a session is running**, for a bounded time, and never just to stay reachable while idle. The bound should come from the tests in section 8.
- **Do not promise delivery to a sleeping device.** Items for it wait (section 5.3).

## 5. Design rules

These are proposals for review. They refine the rules in [architecture.md](architecture.md#locked-devices), and section 5.2 replaces one of them (the push at lock time).

### 5.1 The agent is a per-user process that starts at sign-in

A lock should leave the agent running (reported on Linux, inferred on Windows and macOS). A restart before sign-in does not start it. So the agent is the person's own background process and not a system service:

- **Windows.** Started at sign-in by a Run key or a logon task (documented), or by the Startup folder (inferred; the research did not source it separately). A boot-start LocalSystem service could answer before sign-in, but it would have no user session, no clipboard and no OneDrive client, it cannot work on a machine whose disk is locked by TPM+PIN BitLocker (except on a corporate network that uses Network Unlock, documented), and it would be a large security surface because it runs as SYSTEM. It is not planned. If it is ever offered, it will be a separate install that the person opts in to. One thing could change this decision. On Modern Standby a service might outlast the per-user agent, because services are throttled where the person's processes are suspended (section 3), and that is the research's only sign of a service doing better. W6 therefore runs a service probe next to the per-user probe, so that the per-user decision rests on measurements. The research still recommends per-user as the default. With fast user switching several per-user agents can run at once. They must not bind the same port, and only the agent in the active console session does work that touches the screen (inferred; W12).
- **macOS.** A LaunchAgent installed by the signed app. The one-time Local Network grant is asked for during onboarding, while the person is at the Mac, because launch agents are not exempt from it. Incoming TCP connections and incoming UDP unicast need no grant, but Bonjour registering, browsing and resolving do, so announcing on the local network waits for it (documented). No API shows the alert, and macOS fails to show it for an agent that exits at once, so onboarding triggers it on purpose, for example by connecting a UDP socket to a local address (documented). Until the grant is given the first connection uses the relay, which is inferred to need no grant (M2). macOS 27's launchd refuses property lists that carry the quarantine attribute (documented).
- **Linux.** A `systemd --user` service. Running with nobody logged in ("lingering") gives no display, clipboard or browser, so it would suit only mailbox work (section 5.3). On KDE the compositor lists `ext_data_control_manager_v1`, the protocol that gives background clipboard access, among the interfaces that sandboxed clients (Flatpak, Snap) cannot bind (reported, from source code). So an agent that stages the clipboard on KDE has to be unsandboxed, which decides how it is packaged. Whether a `systemd --user` service may take a blocking inhibitor, or use timers with `WakeSystem=` (generally available only to the system service manager), is unknown (L2).
- **Android.** A foreground service, started while the app is in the foreground. `connectedDevice` is the best-documented type for a service that talks to other devices over a network, and KDE Connect uses it, but choosing it is the researchers' inference and not a rule. Google Play reviews every foreground service type and asks for a declaration and a video, and `specialUse`, which Syncthing-Fork ships with a long justification, is the fallback. For apps that target SDK 37, the local-network permission is asked for during onboarding, and without it the app uses the relay.
- **iOS and iPadOS.** No background agent. The device is reached through push notifications, which need the push gateway of milestone 4. Local network access from the app's extensions follows the app's Local Network grant. While that grant is undetermined, an operation from the background or from an extension is denied without an alert (documented). So onboarding asks for the grant in the foreground, and the notification extension has no local network before then.

After a restart a device cannot be reached until its person signs in (or unlocks the phone once). The apps say so instead of failing quietly.

**How a killed Android listener comes back while the phone stays locked.** Since Android 12 a foreground service cannot be started or restarted from the background, with a few exemptions (documented): the person turned off battery optimisation for the app, the app holds one of the Companion Device Manager permissions, or a high-priority Firebase message arrives. The system can downgrade such a message, and then the app cannot start the service. Three things can also end the listener:

- The person can stop the service from the Task Manager ("Active apps"). The app gets no callback and finds the reason only at its next start.
- A locked private space stops every app in it, notifications included, so Baton installed there cannot receive while the space is locked.
- Phone makers' background killers and Low Power Standby (section 3).

So after a kill with the phone locked the listener may stay dead until the person opens the app, and a push is a helper and not a guarantee. Syncthing-Fork and RustDesk both ask for the battery-optimisation exemption, which the research found to be the pattern for always-on apps. Onboarding names what Baton asks for and why: the exemption, the local-network permission, and the notification. Section 8 tests recovery after a kill while the phone is locked (A11).

### 5.2 Stage before leaving, and do not read a locked device

What a person wants to continue cannot be counted on to be readable once the device locks. iOS and Android do not allow background clipboard reads, GNOME has no sanctioned background access, and on Windows and macOS it is unknown (section 3). So a hand-off starts while the person is there:

- by an explicit gesture that names the item and the device it goes to: a share target, a tray menu, a hotkey, a notification action that asks for authentication, or "send and lock" (section 5.6). The result is a **sent item**; or
- where the platform allows background clipboard reads (X11 and KDE; unknown on Windows and macOS), by staging the clipboard continuously, but only if the person turns that on, and never items that a password manager marks as concealed (see [security.md](security.md)). The result is a **staged item**, which nobody addressed to a device.

Sent items wait in an outbox until their target can be reached (section 5.3). Staged items wait on the device until a paired device asks for them, and section 5.4 says when a locked device answers.

**A sent item needs an unlocked device.** Section 7 rests on a claim: someone who holds only the locked device cannot create an item. A share target, a tray menu, a hotkey and "send and lock" meet it, because a lock screen offers none of them. A notification action, a Quick Settings tile and an App Intent do not meet it by default:

- On Android, a notification action built with `setAuthenticationRequired(true)` makes the system show the keyguard and require authentication before the action runs. With `false` the system decides whether to ask (documented). A tile can be clicked while the lock screen is showing, and its documentation tells a tile that is not safe to run locked to call `unlockAndRun` (documented).
- On iOS, an App Intent runs on a locked device unless its `authenticationPolicy` says otherwise, because the default is `alwaysAllowed` (documented). A notification action waits for an unlock only if it has the `authenticationRequired` option (documented). One without the option can run while the device is locked (inferred; I3 tests it).

So there are two rules:

- **The agent refuses a request to send while the platform says the device is locked.** While the state is unknown, it accepts a send that comes from a gesture that a lock screen does not offer (a share target, a tray menu or menu bar item, a hotkey, "send and lock", a phone action that asks for authentication, or the command line in the person's own session) and refuses a request that comes any other way. A flat refusal on an unknown state would stop some devices from sending at all. On Linux the state is unknown on every desktop where the agent does not trust `LockedHint` (section 6), which includes desktops that use swaylock, i3lock, hyprlock or xsecurelock, because those lockers were not verified to set it. On a Mac the agent has no documented way to read the state when it starts, so it may not know it after a sign-in until the first lock or unlock notification arrives (section 6). The receiving side is different and keeps treating an unknown state as locked (section 5.4). Items already in the outbox are not affected, because they were sent while the device was unlocked. L7 and M13 test both cases.
- **On phones, every action that starts a send asks for authentication first:** `setAuthenticationRequired(true)` on an Android notification action, `unlockAndRun` for a Quick Settings tile, the `authenticationRequired` option on an iOS notification action, and `requiresLocalDeviceAuthentication` as the `authenticationPolicy` of an App Intent. This is the tool that section 5.4 names for the receiving side, used on the sending side. A10, I3 and I5 test it.

**The lock event sends nothing new.** Many locks are idle timeouts with the person still at the desk (inferred; the research has no data on how often), and a push at every lock would deliver content that nobody asked to move. On GNOME and Android nothing would be readable at that moment anyway (section 3). A lock does not hold back what the person already chose, though: sent items keep going out while the device is locked, to the device the person picked.

**A sleep sends nothing new either, and the time before it is short.** That is a separate argument from the lock:

- Linux: a delay inhibitor holds a suspend for five seconds at most by default (documented), and the network may already be going down.
- macOS: an observer of `willSleepNotification` can delay sleep for up to 30 seconds (documented).
- Windows: on Modern Standby the person's processes are suspended a few seconds after the sleep starts (documented), and a program receives `PBT_APMSUSPEND` only if it registered for it (reported).

The agent may use these moments to send the sent items that are still in the outbox, within those limits. It does not read or push anything else.

This replaces the earlier rule that the agent pushes the latest clipboard and tab "when the device is about to lock or sleep". It also overrides a recommendation of the Apple research, which was to flush pending clipboard, link and file announcements at the lock and screen-saver notifications and at `willSleep`. Baton flushes only what the person has already sent, because a staged item was never chosen for a device.

### 5.3 A hand-off must not need the source afterwards

Delivery has three paths, tried in this order:

1. **Direct.** Both devices are awake and can reach each other, on the same network or through a relay. The item is delivered at once.
2. **Sender outbox.** The item waits on the sender until the other device can be reached. This needs the sender to be awake, and it does not need the sender to be unlocked: **a locked agent delivers what the person explicitly sent.** It may dial the named device when that device appears on the network or becomes reachable through the relay, and it dials no other device. The person chose both the item and the target while at the device, so delivering it reveals nothing that they had not decided to share.
3. **Mailbox on the account server** (milestone 4). The server holds the item, encrypted, until the other device comes online. This is the only path that works when the sender has gone to sleep or off before the target appears.

Until the mailbox exists, an item for a device that is off or out of reach is delivered when both devices are awake and reachable at the same time, and the apps say so. A sender that is locked and awake counts as awake. The case that stays open is a laptop that goes to sleep with the item still in the outbox, and the "send and lock" gesture of section 5.6 exists to avoid it: the source is not locked until the other device has the item.

The limits on a locked outbox:

- **Sent items only.** A locked agent does not push staged items. It serves them on request, and only under the opt-in in section 5.4.
- **Only the named device.** The agent never widens an item to another device. Unpairing a device cancels everything queued for it.
- **A lifetime.** An item that has not been delivered within its lifetime is dropped, and the person is told. The proposed default is 24 hours, to be decided before the outbox ships.
- **The receiver decides what happens next.** The receiving device applies its own locked mode and its own policy (section 5.4).
- **Phones.** An iOS or iPadOS app has no agent. After the lock it has about five seconds, which a background task can extend (documented), to finish what the person sent. An item that has not left by then waits until the app runs again, or for the mailbox. An Android foreground service keeps delivering while the phone is locked for as long as it stays alive (section 5.1).

The mailbox is a proposal, drawn from how Magic Wormhole's rendezvous server queues messages for clients that connect later and how Firebase Cloud Messaging stores a message until the device connects (both documented). It needs:

- opaque ciphertext plus minimal routing data, and a sender authenticated by the key from pairing;
- a sequence number for each item and a replay window kept by the receiver, because a relay may not deduplicate or order messages (Magic Wormhole's server does not);
- a short default lifetime, per-device quotas, deletion on acknowledgement, and a purge of a device's mailbox when it is unpaired;
- for large files, an encrypted blob with only its content key and location in the small message, which also fits the 4 KB limit on a Firebase data message;
- no promise to hide metadata. The relay learns who talks to whom, when and how much. Padding, rotating mailbox identifiers and keeping no address logs reduce this and do not remove it. A compromised relay can drop, delay, replay and watch items, and must not be able to read or forge them.

Items for a locked Android phone can be sealed to a key in the Android Keystore that was created with `setUnlockedDeviceRequired(true)`. Public-key operations stay allowed while the phone is locked, and the private key is usable only after unlock (documented). The same page lists bugs on Android 12 to 14: the key can be unusable without a secure lock screen, can be deleted when the lock screen is removed, and is not re-authorised by non-strong biometrics such as face unlock. Items sealed to such a key can then be lost or not opened. So Baton uses the unlock-gated key only where test A9 passes (versions, lock-screen types and biometric classes). The receiver reports an item it cannot open, so the sender can send it again under the ordinary key. A phone that fails the test uses the ordinary key, which stays readable at the lock screen. Section 5.9 says more.

### 5.4 Locked mode on the receiving side

- The agent learns the lock state from the platform's events (section 6), reads it once when it starts, because it may start inside a locked session, and reads it again after every resume. **If the state cannot be known, it acts as if the device were locked** for received items. Section 5.2 treats sending differently, so that a device whose locker never reports its state can still send. iOS has no public API for it. On Linux the logind `LockedHint` reads false until a locker sets it, so the state is unknown on every desktop where the agent does not trust the property (section 6).
- While locked, the agent accepts items from paired devices into the inbox and does nothing else with them. No link is opened, no clipboard is written, and no file is opened. Policies that open or apply items automatically are suspended until unlock.
- It shows a notification that names the sender and hides the content where the platform lets it (Android visibility settings and actions that require authentication, the iOS preview setting and actions that require authentication). On desktops the person's own settings decide what the lock screen shows, and Baton cannot override them: Windows 11 has "Show notifications on the lock screen" and a separate setting for reminders and VoIP calls that is on by default, and macOS has per-user settings including "Always show previews (even in the login window)" (documented). So the rule is that **a notification posted while the device is locked carries no content**, only the sender and the fact that something arrived. On iOS, if the notification service extension does not finish in time, the system shows the original content of the push (documented). The alert text inside the push itself is therefore the placeholder, and the extension may improve on it, never the other way round. On unlock the inbox is shown, and what the person's policy allows is opened then.
- It starts no hand-off of its own, but it finishes the ones the person started: it delivers sent items to the devices they were addressed to, dialing them if needed (section 5.3). It answers pings and `Hello`. It answers a request from a paired device for the items that were sent to that device. It serves staged items, which nobody addressed, to a paired device only if the person turned that on for that device, which is off by default.
- An open pairing offer ends when the device locks. Pairing needs a person to compare the codes and approve, so it is never completed on a locked device.

Why: on Windows a handler started while locked opens on a desktop nobody sees, and GNOME hides every window in lock mode, so a link that "opened" is invisible and anything it did goes unobserved. Sending is different: it shows nothing on the lock screen, and it goes only where the person pointed it. A stolen device that is locked but running also holds the device key, which section 7 covers.

### 5.5 Unlocking

Baton has no code that calls the platform's unlock function, and it asks no device to unlock itself. The one case that is designed, in [ADR 0013](adr/0013-unlock-from-a-phone.md) and not built yet, is a Mac that unlocks for a paired iPhone or iPad. The person sets it up at the Mac, for that phone, and types the password of the Mac there. The Mac keeps the password in a box that only a key sealed to the phone's Secure Enclave opens, and that key opens only for the face of the person. When the Mac is locked in a session that keeps it dark and held, the person taps Unlock on the phone, the face opens the key, the phone hands it back, and the Mac puts the password in a new box under a new key, so that a copy of the key opens nothing afterwards (the ADR names the two cases in which it does), types its own password into the lock screen, once, and looks whether it worked. A Mac that is asleep, shut or just restarted is out of reach. Whether a session can start on a Mac that is already locked is not known (U11 of the ADR); if it can, the unlock works there too. The disk of the Mac alone, the phone alone and a passcode each open nothing, the person at the Mac is told of each unlock, and a session that was unlocked ends locked, unless the person at the phone chose, when they ended it, to leave the Mac as it is ([ADR 0014](adr/0014-lock-the-mac-or-leave-it.md)). It is off by default and made at the Mac by no message.

The rest of this section is about what stays out.

On Linux any process of the same user can call logind `Session.Unlock` without a password, and GNOME Shell and KDE's locker both obey it (reported, to be confirmed by test L6). A compromised agent could therefore unlock the machine. KDE Connect has a remote-unlock feature that is on by default (reported; L13 checks it). The research also found that on Windows a SYSTEM-level helper can type credentials at the lock screen. Baton will build neither, and the unlock of a Mac does not rest on either: it types the password that the person gave, and it needs the face of the person on a paired phone. A lock is not a barrier against malware that runs as the person, and Baton does not weaken it further.

### 5.6 Lock the old device after a hand-off (optional)

The person's worry is leaving the first device unlocked. Baton can address it in two ways, and both lock only after the other device has acknowledged the item:

- **Send and lock.** One gesture on the source: send these items to that device, wait for its acknowledgement, then lock this device. The lock is a local act on the device the person is using, so it needs no request to another device and no setting beyond the gesture. With a reachable target this meets the requirement in milestone 1, without waiting for the mailbox. If no acknowledgement arrives within a short wait (a setting, with a default that the tests should set), the app says so and asks whether to lock anyway. Locking anyway leaves the item in the outbox, where the locked agent keeps trying (section 5.3) for as long as the device stays awake.
- **Lock on request.** The receiving device asks the sending device to lock itself once it has acknowledged a hand-off. This is for hand-offs that were not started with "send and lock", and it is the setting described in the rules below.

| Platform | How a lock is started |
| --- | --- |
| Windows | `LockWorkStation()` from the agent in the person's session. It is asynchronous, a credential provider can deny it, and it needs a caller on the interactive desktop (documented), so it does not work from a service (inferred). |
| Linux | logind `Session.Lock()`, allowed for the session owner without a password and honoured by GNOME Shell and KDE's locker (reported). |
| macOS | No function is documented as a lock. Two public routes may work, and M12 tests them: putting the display to sleep (`pmset displaysleepnow`; its man page says it causes the display to go to sleep immediately, and names root only for changing settings, so it probably needs none, which is unknown) and starting the screen saver. Either one locks the Mac if the person's Lock Screen setting asks for a password as soon as the screen saver or sleep begins (the setting is documented, and that these two calls start the lock is inferred). With a delay in that setting the Mac stays unlocked, no lock notification arrives, and the confirmed-lock rule below reports it. Private functions exist and other tools use them. Baton will not depend on one. |
| Android | `DevicePolicyManager.lockNow()` for an app that is an active device administrator, or an accessibility service's lock-screen action (documented). Both are privileged. The accessibility route has Google Play policy consequences, and A12 tests whether it works on the lock screen. Whether Android's deprecation of some device-admin policies affects the first was not checked. So this is offered only as an opt-in. |
| iOS and iPadOS | None. |

The rules:

- Lock on request is off by default and set on the device that gets locked, per paired device ("let this device lock me"). "Send and lock" is the person's own gesture on that device and needs no setting.
- It happens only after the item is acknowledged. On GNOME a lock closes remote-access sessions immediately, so a transfer that depends on one would die.
- **A lock is confirmed, not assumed.** After asking for a lock the agent waits for the platform's lock signal (`WTS_SESSION_LOCK` on Windows, `LockedHint` on Linux, `com.apple.screenIsLocked` on macOS; section 6). It tells the person the device is locked only when that signal arrives. If it does not arrive within a few seconds, the app reports that the lock was refused or failed, on the device the person is now using, because they may already have walked away. On a Linux desktop where the agent does not trust `LockedHint` (section 6), a missing signal does not show that the lock failed, so the app says that it could not confirm the lock. A signal that does arrive counts as the first true reading for learned trust. W13 tests the sequence on Windows and M12 on macOS.
- The trigger is the acknowledgement from a device that is unlocked and in use. Proximity is not a trigger: it needs Bluetooth signal-strength heuristics that were not researched.
- It is rate limited and the person is told when it happens. A compromised paired device could lock the person out repeatedly, which is a nuisance and not a leak.
- There is no opposite command. Locking can only make a device safer, which is why a paired device can be allowed to ask for it. The unlock of section 5.5 is a feature of its own, with a setting of its own and the face of the person, and nothing here causes it.

### 5.7 Waking a sleeping device (optional, best effort)

- **Wake-on-LAN.** A magic packet is unauthenticated and sent in the clear, it needs a sender on the same link (or a helper on that network), and whether it works depends on the hardware and driver. It wakes the hardware only. The machine is expected to come back at its lock screen, but that is inferred and is tested on every desktop (section 8), because an old device that wakes up unlocked is what the person is worried about. A network wake on a Mac may be only a Dark Wake. KDE suspends again after 30 seconds unless user activity is registered. An Android sender needs the local-network permission when it targets SDK 37 (documented). It is offered as a setting, never relied on.
- **Sleep proxy (multicast DNS).** Apple TVs, HomePods and AirPort base stations act as sleep proxies for Macs (reported). The protocol is not limited to them. The documentation of a GPL-3 tool says that it runs on Windows 8 to 11, Linux and macOS and speaks the standard Sleep Proxy protocol, both as a proxy that accepts registrations and wakes hosts and as a client that registers its own services before suspending, including with an Apple TV, a HomePod or an AirPort base station (reported, from its documentation read through a summarising tool and not from its code; the notes name the tool and give the pages). A client and a server therefore exist for Windows and Linux, and because of the license nothing can be taken from them. Apple's proxies end a registration after 24 hours and wake the host then (reported, from the same documentation). If it works, a Windows or Linux agent registers its service with a sleep proxy before the device sleeps, and a paired device wakes it just by connecting, with no magic packet to send and no MAC address to share. An awake Baton device on the same network could act as the proxy for its paired devices. Both are ideas for the wake helpers of milestone 4 and neither is tested. They still need a network card that wakes on a magic packet and a proxy on the same link, so they stay best effort. W6 and L4 test the registration.
- **Push.** A high-priority Firebase message can wake an Android device if it produces a visible notification, and an alert push reaches an iPhone or iPad. Both need the push gateway of milestone 4.
- **Desktops.** A suspended Linux desktop has its user processes frozen, so the routes are Wake-on-LAN, a hardware alarm, or the person opening the lid (documented, inferred). A systemd timer with `WakeSystem=` can wake it, but that is generally available only to the system service manager (documented). On Windows the research names timer-triggered Microsoft Store background tasks as the sanctioned way to run code during Modern Standby (they run only on AC power, at most every 15 minutes), and whether a package from outside the Store could use them was not researched. On macOS nothing was found. So no push route is established for desktops. The mailbox in section 5.3 is the real answer and Wake-on-LAN is a helper.
- A wake never unlocks.

### 5.8 Streaming from a device that stays locked (milestone 5)

No documented way for a third party captures a locked session on any platform examined (section 3). Apple's iPhone Mirroring is built around a locked device and keeps the iPhone locked (documented), but it works only between an iPhone and a Mac and its protocol cannot be reused. Window streaming therefore starts from these rules:

- **The first releases need the source unlocked while the stream runs**, with a visible notice on it. When the stream ends, or the hand-off completes, Baton can lock the source (section 5.6).
- **A separate session** (a second login session or a virtual display) gives the viewer a new desktop and not the windows the person left. On Linux, Chrome Remote Desktop and GNOME Remote Desktop's headless mode do this, and so does KDE's remote-access mode, which is known only from the master branch of its source code and a blog post, with its handling of the lock unfinished (reported). On Windows it is not established that Chrome Remote Desktop opens a new session and does not re-attach the existing one (W10), and a virtual display is a hypothesis only (W11). Continuity would then come from files, links and application profiles, which Baton carries (sections 5.2 and 5.3). It is heavy, and it has weaknesses of its own: KDE's mode deliberately leaves the person's session unlocked behind the login screen, so someone at the keyboard may reach it (reported, to be tested). A separate session as the same user is also not isolated from a compromised agent.
- **A privacy mode** that blanks the physical screen and blocks local input while streaming has a public-API version on macOS (RustDesk's approach, inferred from a summarised read of its source code). A person at the keyboard can defeat it, so it is a convenience and never a security feature.
- **If the source must stay locked while it streams**, the pattern the Apple research offers is the one in iPhone Mirroring: a pinned, hardware-backed device identity, an authentication step on the controlling device, a time-boxed session, and visible indicators on the source. It is a design option for milestone 5 and not a promise.
- **Nothing captures the lock screen itself**, and the one thing typed into it is the password of the unlock in section 5.5.
- The apps promise no streaming from a locked device until the tests in section 8 show it works.

### 5.9 Keys and the lock screen

- **The device identity key must stay readable at the lock screen**, or the agent could not authenticate or receive: Keychain items of the after-first-unlock class and iOS files protected until first user authentication, Android Keystore keys without `setUnlockedDeviceRequired`, and a different home for the key where a Secret Service collection locks with the screen. [security.md](security.md) lists this under key storage. On macOS it is not yet known to work. The research's evidence for the after-first-unlock class covers iOS only, and nothing tests whether a LaunchAgent can read its key while the screen is locked (M11). The login keychain can also be set to lock after inactivity or on sleep, which the research did not check (unverified), so the key's home on macOS stays an open question until M11 has run.
- **Content keys for queued items may be tied to unlock where the platform has a control for it.** On Android, items can be sealed to a key that opens only on an unlocked phone, on the versions where that works (section 5.3). The cost is that a locked phone cannot show a preview of such an item, only that one arrived, which matches the notification rules in section 5.4. On iOS the keys that the notification extension needs stay in the after-first-unlock class, and before the first unlock after a restart the extension shows a generic placeholder and the item is processed after the unlock. No equivalent control was found on desktops, and whether GNOME Keyring or KWallet stay unlocked while the screen is locked was not checked.

### 5.10 Where this goes beyond what exists

As far as the research could tell, and without having tested any of these tools:

- RustDesk, AnyDesk, Parsec, KDE Connect and Chrome Remote Desktop treat a locked remote machine as something to get past: stored credentials, remote unlock, privacy modes, or a separate session. Baton treats locked as a mode to respect: stage before leaving, queue, quarantine, and unlock only for the person, after their face, on a Mac they set up for it (section 5.5).
- Sealing queued items to a key whose private half can be used only while the receiving phone is unlocked (the key itself persists) is an idea I did not come across in the hand-off tools examined (Magic Wormhole, Taildrop, KDE Connect), though I did not search them for it. It rests on a documented Android feature that has reported bugs on Android 12 to 14 (section 5.3), and it is untested.
- Locking the old device as part of a hand-off is related to RustDesk's option to lock a host when a remote session ends, to Windows Dynamic Lock, which locks a PC when a paired phone's signal falls (documented), and to the "Lock PC" action of Link to Windows, which locks a PC from an Android phone and has no unlock (weak sources). None of them is tied to the delivery of something. "Send and lock", which locks the source only after the other device has acknowledged the item, is the new part, and its value is unproven.

### 5.11 Where each rule lands

- **Milestone 1.** The per-user agent on desktops. Lock detection on Windows, macOS and Linux, with unknown treated as locked (on Linux, wherever the agent does not trust `LockedHint`). Locked mode for received items. Resume handling. The sender outbox, which a locked agent keeps delivering, the refusal to send while the device is locked (and, while its state is unknown, of every request except a gesture that a lock screen does not offer), and "send and lock" on Windows and Linux, and on macOS if M12 shows that the route works. The tests in section 8, with a small probe program built for them.
- **Milestone 2.** The Android foreground service and its permission. The apps' share targets as the explicit "continue on" gesture, with authentication on every phone action that starts a send. The iOS app works while it is in the foreground.
- **When the agent exists.** Lock on request from the receiving device, on Windows and Linux first.
- **Milestone 4.** The mailbox, the push gateway for Firebase and Apple push, wake helpers (Wake-on-LAN, and perhaps a sleep proxy registration, section 5.7), and unlock-gated content keys on Android.
- **Milestone 5.** Window streaming under the rules in section 5.8.

## 6. Detecting lock, sleep and resume

Events are the source of truth, and a read of the current state corrects them when the agent starts and after every resume.

**Windows** (the agent runs in the person's session):

- Lock and unlock: create a hidden top-level window and register it with `WTSRegisterSessionNotification(hwnd, NOTIFY_FOR_THIS_SESSION)`, then handle `WM_WTSSESSION_CHANGE` with `WTS_SESSION_LOCK` and `WTS_SESSION_UNLOCK` (documented). Chromium and Electron do the same (reported). Whether a message-only window receives these messages is unknown, so use a top-level one.
- State at start: `WTSQuerySessionInformation` with `WTSSessionInfoEx` returns a lock flag. There is no function that simply asks whether the workstation is locked (documented).
- Sleep and resume: `RegisterSuspendResumeNotification` on the window (`PBT_APMSUSPEND`, `PBT_APMRESUMEAUTOMATIC`), or `PowerRegisterSuspendResumeNotification` with a callback when there is no window (documented).
- Display and presence: `RegisterPowerSettingNotification` for `GUID_CONSOLE_DISPLAY_STATE` and `GUID_SESSION_USER_PRESENCE` (documented).
- Fast user switching and Remote Desktop: `WTS_CONSOLE_CONNECT`, `WTS_CONSOLE_DISCONNECT`, `WTS_REMOTE_CONNECT` and `WTS_REMOTE_DISCONNECT` (documented).
- Power model: `AoAc` in `SYSTEM_POWER_CAPABILITIES` from `CallNtPowerInformation` is true on Modern Standby (documented).

**macOS:**

- Lock and unlock: the distributed notifications `com.apple.screenIsLocked` and `com.apple.screenIsUnlocked`, and `com.apple.screensaver.didstart` and `.didstop`. They are undocumented. Chromium, Electron and Hammerspoon have used them for a long time (reported), and nobody checked macOS 26 or 27.
- Fast user switching: `NSWorkspace.sessionDidResignActiveNotification` (documented). It does not fire for the screen lock.
- Sleep and wake: `NSWorkspace.willSleepNotification` (the observer may delay sleep by up to 30 seconds), `didWakeNotification`, `screensDidSleepNotification`, and `IORegisterForSystemPower` (documented).
- Polling: `CGSessionCopyCurrentDictionary()` for session ownership and `CGDisplayIsAsleep` for the display. The key that says the screen is locked, `CGSSessionScreenIsLocked`, is private and was seen only in community scripts.
- State at start: no documented source. The agent can read the private key or wait for the first lock or unlock notification, and until then the state is unknown. M13 tests what an agent started at sign-in and inside a locked session sees.

**Linux:**

- Lock: the logind session property `LockedHint`, which the locker writes. GNOME and KDE set it (reported). Read it from the session's own path (`/org/freedesktop/login1/session/<id>`) and watch property changes there. Whether the `auto` alias delivers change signals is uncertain: a comment in KDE Connect's source says it does not, and other code reads the property through it (reported; L11 settles it). The `Lock` and `Unlock` signals are requests to the locker and not state, so they say nothing about whether the device is locked. `org.gnome.ScreenSaver.ActiveChanged` (GNOME, which can trail the real lock by about 0.3 seconds) and `org.freedesktop.ScreenSaver.ActiveChanged` (KDE) are also available. Other lockers (xsecurelock, i3lock, swaylock, hyprlock) were not verified to set `LockedHint`.
- **`LockedHint` has no value for unknown.** The property is a boolean (documented). Logind's source creates every session with it false and changes it when something calls `SetLockedHint` (reported, from source code). On a desktop whose locker never calls that method it reads false while the screen is locked, so a false reading is not proof that the device is unlocked, and unknown has to be the agent's own decision.
- **When the agent trusts `LockedHint`.** The agent trusts the property on a desktop verified to set it, which today means GNOME and KDE (reported), and L7 adds to that list. It finds the desktop from `XDG_CURRENT_DESKTOP`, a colon-separated list that the login manager sets from the session file (documented), and, if the agent's environment does not have it, from the session's `Desktop` property, which logind describes as the desktop environment running in the session, if known (documented). The two do not have the same form: one is a list, and the other is a single identifier that logind only recommends should use the list's spelling (documented). A list can also name GNOME without the desktop running GNOME Shell's locker (inferred). So the agent compares the whole value with the ones that L7 records for each desktop on the list, and never a word inside it. On any other desktop, or when none of these sources gives a value, the state is unknown. That is what makes received items wait in the inbox (section 5.4) and limits sends (section 5.2).
- **A user service may start before the variable exists.** A `systemd --user` service gets the environment block of the user manager when it starts, and that block holds what the session has imported into it by then (documented). A service that starts earlier never sees a variable that is imported later (inferred). So the agent does not rely on its own environment alone. When its own environment lacks `XDG_CURRENT_DESKTOP`, it reads the variable from the `Environment` property of the user manager over D-Bus (documented for the manager interface, and L7 checks it on the user manager), which does not depend on when the agent started. Starting the agent from `graphical-session.target` would not settle it by itself. The documentation says that this target is active while a graphical session runs and that `graphical-session-pre.target` is for services that set up the environment, and neither page says that a session imports this variable before the target is reached (not documented). L7 records what the manager holds and when it got it.
- **Learned trust.** Without it, a desktop whose locker writes the property but is not on the list would leave every received item waiting in the inbox for the person to open. So once the agent has seen the property turn true in a session, that locker evidently writes it, and the agent trusts the property for the rest of that session. The gap that follows is not specific to learned trust. On any desktop where the agent trusts the property, listed or learned, it cannot tell which locker ran. A second locker that does not write the property (one started by hand, for example) leaves the property false, and the device is counted as unlocked while it is locked. Learned trust adds desktops to the ones where this can happen. This is inferred, and L14 measures how wide the gap is.
- Sleep: `PrepareForSleep` on logind's manager, together with a `sleep` delay inhibitor (documented).

**Android:**

- Screen state: `ACTION_SCREEN_OFF` and `ACTION_SCREEN_ON` through a receiver registered at run time, because the manifest cannot declare them. `ACTION_USER_PRESENT` when the lock screen has gone. Poll `KeyguardManager.isDeviceLocked()`, which honours Smart Lock. The listener APIs need a permission that ordinary apps cannot get (documented).
- Storage: `UserManager.isUserUnlocked()`, `ACTION_USER_UNLOCKED`, and `ACTION_LOCKED_BOOT_COMPLETED` for components marked `directBootAware` (documented).
- Power: `PowerManager.isDeviceIdleMode()` and `isLowPowerStandbyEnabled()`, with their broadcasts (documented).

**iOS and iPadOS:**

- There is no public API for the lock state. `UIApplication.protectedDataWillBecomeUnavailableNotification`, `protectedDataDidBecomeAvailableNotification` and `isProtectedDataAvailable` say when protected files become unreadable, which follows a lock on a device with a passcode (documented). Scene transitions happen at a lock too, but they are not a lock signal by themselves.

## 7. A locked device that is stolen while the agent runs

The agent holds the device key while the device is locked, and the network is up. What that allows, and what limits it:

| Attack | Finding | Response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Probe the running agent over the network | A lock stops nothing, and Android keeps its foreground service alive. | In place: mutual authentication in the handshake, nothing served to unpaired devices, and rate limits (see [security.md](security.md)). |
| Use the stolen device's key to push links or files to the person's other devices | The agent can sign while locked. | Receivers open nothing on their own unless the person opted in, and show the sender. Unpairing the device on any other device ends its access. Proposed: per-device rights (links yes, files into a quarantine only) and trust that expires after a number of days without contact. |
| Items waiting in the outbox of a stolen device | A locked agent delivers sent items, so they leave the device on their own, and until then a thief holds them. | They go only to the device the person chose, and that device applies its own locked mode and policy. Unpairing it cancels them, and a lifetime (proposed: 24 hours) drops the rest. Someone who has only the locked device cannot create new items, because the agent refuses to send while the device is locked and the phone actions that send ask for authentication (section 5.2). |
| Send something from the lock screen of a stolen phone | A notification action, a Quick Settings tile or an App Intent can run on a locked phone unless it asks for authentication (documented, except for an iOS notification action, which is inferred). | Every action that starts a send asks for authentication. The agent refuses a send while the device is locked, and while its state is unknown it accepts one only from a gesture that a lock screen does not offer (section 5.2). A10, I3 and I5 test the phone actions, and L7 and M13 the unknown state. |
| Read items queued for the stolen device | The agent can decrypt while it runs. | Proposed: on Android a key that opens only on an unlocked phone, a short lifetime, and a purge on unpairing. No desktop equivalent was found. |
| Turn the agent into an unlocker | Any process of the same Linux user can call the unlock method. | Baton calls no unlock method (section 5.5), and the Linux agent runs as a hardened user unit. |
| Use a lost or stolen paired phone to unlock the person's Mac | A phone that was set up to unlock a Mac can be tapped by whoever holds it. | The key that opens the box opens only for the face of the person, with no passcode, and a new face ends it. The Mac replaces the box with a new one at every unlock, so a copy of a key that was used opens nothing afterwards and every unlock needs the face. Two cases are not covered: a Mac that cannot keep its new box keeps the old one, which the key it was given still opens, and a changed phone app that opened a key and did not send it can use that key for one unlock without a face (ADR 0013, Consequences). The Mac unlocks only in a private session, for a phone that the person set up at the Mac, types one password for each tap, and tells the person. Unpairing the phone, or turning the setting off, deletes the box (section 5.5). The Mac takes the word of the phone that its key is in the Secure Enclave and cannot check it, so a phone app that was changed before the setup is not stopped by the face. A Mac that locked before the session began can be unlocked too, if a session can start on it (U11 of the ADR). |
| Leak incoming content on the lock screen | Android visibility is user-controlled, GNOME hides notifications in lock mode, and KDE was not checked (L12). Windows 11 and macOS have settings of the person's own that can show notifications, and previews, on the lock screen. | No content in notifications posted while locked, on any platform. On iOS the placeholder is in the push itself (section 5.4). |
| Act on content that arrived while locked | Opening links or saving files unseen is a drive-by risk. | Locked mode is receive and quarantine only (section 5.4). |
| Attacks that skip the lock (boot from USB, DMA, cold boot) | Out of scope for the agent. Android's credential-protected storage stays available from the first unlock until a reboot. | Full-disk encryption on laptops, no unprotected long-term keys, and the knowledge that a phone unlocked once since boot is more exposed than a rebooted one. |
| Compromise of the relay, or traffic analysis | Magic Wormhole's design is the precedent. | End-to-end encryption and signatures, padding, minimal retention (section 5.3). |
| Wake a stolen sleeping laptop | The magic packet is unauthenticated. | It only wakes the machine. The machine is expected to return to its lock screen (inferred, tested on every desktop in section 8), and the risk is low if it does. |
| Lock the person out repeatedly from a compromised paired device | KDE Connect has a remote lock. | Rate limit lock requests and tell the person (section 5.6). |

## 8. To verify on real hardware

None of the behaviour in section 3 marked inferred or unknown should be relied on until these have been run. Record the operating system and build, the power model, AC or battery, wired or Wi-Fi, the disk-encryption mode, and whether the display was on or off.

A small **probe** program does the observing, and it is part of the first milestone. It writes a line with a monotonic timestamp every second, logs the lock, unlock, sleep, resume and display events that section 6 describes, and answers a TCP and a UDP echo, while a second machine probes it once a second. Each test below is "run the probe, do this, and look for gaps". The [research notes](research/locked-devices/README.md) number their tests differently (T for Windows and Apple, V for Linux and Android). Where a test below comes from one of them, its id is given.

**Windows**

- W1. Read the power model and standby networking (`powercfg /a`, the wake-armed devices, adapter power settings). Decides which column of the table applies to each machine.
- W2. Liveness while locked: lock, let the display go off, wait 15 minutes, on AC and on battery. Then set a short sleep timeout and see whether the heartbeat stops before the machine visibly sleeps. Decides the first row of the Windows table and whether a display that is off suspends processes on Modern Standby.
- W3. Clipboard while locked: set it from the agent and listen for changes, then unlock and paste.
- W4. Opening a link and a file while locked, with each browser already running and not running.
- W5. Capture while locked with Desktop Duplication, Windows.Graphics.Capture, `PrintWindow` and `BitBlt`, as the person and as SYSTEM, for a window that animates. **Decides whether streaming from a locked device is possible at all.**
- W6. Modern Standby liveness and wake: enter sleep by the Start menu, the lid and the idle timeout, on AC and on battery, with and without a power request held, then send a Wake-on-LAN packet and see whether the agent runs while the display stays off, and whether the session comes back locked. Run a service probe (LocalSystem, started at boot) next to the per-user probe and compare how each is throttled or suspended. Hold the request once as `PowerSetRequest` and once as `SetThreadExecutionState`, and see which of them ends five minutes after the sleep timeout on battery (T06 in the notes). With a sleep proxy on the same link, also register the machine's service before it sleeps and see whether a connection to that service wakes it (section 5.7).
- W7. The same on an S3 machine, including how long the machine stays awake after a Wake-on-LAN packet and whether the session comes back locked.
- W8. Before sign-in: whether a boot-start service is reachable after a restart and after a Fast Startup shutdown, with no BitLocker, TPM-only and TPM+PIN. Also hibernate and resume (with TPM+PIN the PIN is asked first): whether the per-user agent survives and its network recovers (T17 in the notes).
- W9. Lock detection over 20 cycles each of Win+L, Ctrl+Alt+Del then Lock, a lock from the agent, an idle lock, Dynamic Lock, a user switch, a Remote Desktop connection and sleep with resume, comparing a top-level window with a message-only one.
- W10. Loopback Remote Desktop: while signed in at the console with a window open, connect to the same machine's loopback address as the same user (Remote Desktop on, network level authentication off on the test machine). Record whether the existing session, with the open window, is attached to the Remote Desktop window or a new empty session is created, and what the console shows. Optionally repeat with Chrome Remote Desktop in curtain mode from another device. Decides what milestone 5 can promise on Windows (T15 in the notes).
- W11. Virtual display while locked: on a machine with Secure Boot on and no test signing, install a signed community virtual display driver and note any warning. Move an animated test window onto the virtual monitor and run the capture harness of W5 against it through lock, sign-out and sign-in, sleep and resume, and fast user switching. Record whether the monitor persists, frames continue and the window survives. Until then it is a hypothesis only (T16 in the notes).
- W12. Fast user switching: user A signed in with the probe, lock A, then sign in as B at the console with B's probe listening on the same port. Record A's heartbeat, port conflicts, the active console session id and the session events on both agents, and whether A's clipboard and file reads still work while A is disconnected. Decides the rule for several agents in section 5.1 (T18 in the notes).
- W13. Lock after hand-off: from the per-user probe call `LockWorkStation` on command and record the result and the delay until `WTS_SESSION_LOCK` arrives. Call it from a service in session 0 and record the result (a failure is expected). Call it when the device is already locked. If the test machine has a credential provider that can deny the call, record what the call returns. Decides whether the confirmed-lock rule in section 5.6 can rest on the signal (T21 in the notes).
- Also: reading files while locked, including OneDrive online-only files and EFS files; toasts on the lock screen; input while locked (T13 in the notes); start-up after an update restart; what the "Lock PC" action of Link to Windows does with the PC asleep or offline (T25 in the notes).

**macOS**

- M1. Liveness and network while locked: a LaunchAgent that logs a heartbeat and echoes on TCP and UDP, for 30 minutes with the display on, asleep and left to sleep, on AC and on battery, with a second account switched out, and with a screen saver and a password. Run it three ways to see whether App Nap throttles an agent that has no windows: holding nothing, holding a `ProcessInfo` activity, and holding a power assertion. Pass: no gap above two seconds and every probe answered (T1 in the notes).
- M2. Local Network grant: install with SMAppService, lock before the first LAN operation, and see what is denied, whether a prompt waits for the unlock, and whether the relay works with the grant denied.
- M3. Assertions, `caffeinate` and the lid: which assertion each creates, and which cases still sleep on AC and on battery, with the lid closed with and without an external display.
- M4. Wake-on-LAN, Sleep Proxy and scheduled wake on Apple silicon, on Ethernet and on Wi-Fi, and whether the Mac then comes back at its lock screen or only does a Dark Wake without a session.
- M5. FileVault restart: what is reachable before the password is entered, and the SSH unlock on macOS 26 and later.
- M6. Pasteboard from the agent while locked, set and read, on macOS 27.0, 27.1 and the 27.2 beta, and Universal Clipboard to a locked Mac.
- M7. Opening a link and posting a notification while locked, with each preview setting.
- M8. Capture while locked with ScreenCaptureKit and the older capture APIs, with the display on, asleep, kept awake and under a screen saver. Decides whether streaming from a locked Mac is possible.
- M9. Input while locked through CGEvent and the Accessibility API, aimed at the window of a test app. Baton never types into a lock screen, so nothing is aimed at it (T10 in the notes).
- M10. Lock detection: log the distributed notifications, the workspace notifications and the session keys through a Control-Command-Q lock, a hot corner, the screen saver, lid close and open, Touch ID, Apple Watch unlock and a restart, on macOS 26 and 27.
- M11. The key at a locked screen: a LaunchAgent that reads a key from the Keychain (after-first-unlock class) while the screen is locked, after the display sleeps, and after a sleep and wake, and with the login keychain set to lock after inactivity or on sleep. Decides where the device key lives on macOS (section 5.9).
- M12. Locking the Mac from the agent: run `pmset displaysleepnow` and start the screen saver (for example with `open -a ScreenSaverEngine`) as the signed-in person with no extra privilege, with the Lock Screen setting on an immediate password, on each delay and on never. Record whether the Mac locks, whether `com.apple.screenIsLocked` arrives and how long after the call, whether `displaysleepnow` needs root on Apple silicon, and what a laptop with an external display or with the lid closed does. Decides the macOS row of section 5.6 (see also M10).
- M13. Lock state at start: start the agent at sign-in and inside an already locked session, on macOS 26 and 27, and record what it reads (the private `CGSSessionScreenIsLocked` key, the dictionary from `CGSessionCopyCurrentDictionary`, the distributed notifications) and how long the state stays unknown. While it is unknown, a send from the menu bar item, a hotkey and the command line should be accepted and any other request refused. Decides how the agent reads the state at start (section 5.4) and the sending rule in section 5.2.

**Linux**

- L1. Lid close against inhibitors (`sleep` block against `handle-lid-switch`) on GNOME and KDE, with and without an external monitor.
- L2. Idle suspend while locked, and whether a `systemd --user` service may take delay and block inhibitors or arm a timer with `WakeSystem=`. With auto-suspend set to one minute, time how long a UDP echo keeps answering.
- L3. Reachability while locked: a UDP echo as a user service, `loginctl lock-session`, screen blanking, one probe every 30 seconds for two hours on GNOME, KDE and one X11 session, with Wi-Fi power save on and off.
- L4. Wake-on-LAN and Wake-on-WLAN from s2idle and S3, how long KDE stays awake after a network wake, and whether the session comes back locked. Also whether registering the machine's service with a sleep proxy before it suspends lets a connection wake it (section 5.7).
- L5. What a `PrepareForSleep` hook can still send before the network goes down.
- L6. Same-user unlock: `loginctl unlock-session` over SSH and from a user service, on GNOME and KDE. Confirms section 5.5.
- L7. Whether `LockedHint` is set by `xdg-screensaver lock` on KDE, Xfce, Cinnamon and i3 with `xss-lock`, and by swaylock, i3lock and hyprlock. On each locker where it is not set, whether a send from the tray, a hotkey and the command line is accepted while the state is unknown and a request that comes any other way is refused (section 5.2). On every desktop it checks, record the exact values of `XDG_CURRENT_DESKTOP` and of the session's `Desktop` property, whether the `Environment` property of the user manager holds the first and when it got it, and whether a `systemd --user` service that starts before the session imports its environment sees it, because the list of desktops where the hint is trusted is made of those values (section 6).
- L8. GNOME: confirm that screen casts and remote-control sessions are closed on lock and refused while locked. Whether a Shell extension can use the clipboard in the lock-screen session mode. What opening a URL does while locked.
- L9. KDE: capture, input and clipboard while locked, and whether KDE's remote-access mode leaves an unlocked session reachable from a text console at the keyboard.
- L10. X11 lockers: clipboard, synthetic input and capture while locked.
- L11. Lock signal timing: watch the screensaver interface (GNOME or KDE), logind's `Lock` signal and `LockedHint` while locking by hotkey, by idle timeout and by lid close. Record their order and delay, the effect of GNOME's `lock-delay`, and whether property changes are emitted from the `auto` alias or only from the session's own path. Decides which signal Baton trusts (section 6; V-L8 in the notes).
- L12. KDE: opening a URL while locked (`xdg-open`, `kioclient exec`) and a notification sent while locked; record what is visible. Decides how section 5.4 treats KDE (V-K4 in the notes).
- L13. KDE Connect remote unlock, as a check of the tool and not of Baton: two paired Plasma machines, lock one and use the other to unlock it. Record whether a password is asked and whether the feature is on right after pairing. Confirms the claim in section 5.5 (V-T2 in the notes).
- L14. The second-locker gap (section 6). On each desktop where the hint is trusted, listed or learned, try to start a locker that does not write the hint (i3lock or swaylock started by hand, for example), and record whether the desktop lets it take the screen at all and, if it does, what the agent counts the device as after that lock. The gap is as wide as the set of desktops where this works. Decides whether the agent needs a second signal before it acts on received items, and whether learned trust stays in the design.

**Android**

- A1. Whether a foreground service keeps the network in Doze: a UDP echo, forced deep Doze, charging and not, against a control with the service stopped. Settles a conflict between the documentation and AOSP code.
- A2. The default of Low Power Standby on Android 16 and 17 on a Pixel and a Samsung, and whether it breaks a foreground-service listener.
- A3. Latency of a packet to a naturally sleeping phone after 5, 15, 30, 60 and 120 minutes, with keep-alives every 30 seconds, 5 minutes and 15 minutes, and when the router or the phone drops the path.
- A4. Battery cost of the service over 12 hours, in three arms.
- A5. The local-network permission on Android 17: the same app built for SDK 36 and 37, granted, denied and revoked, for inbound and outbound UDP and TCP, broadcast and mDNS.
- A6. Clipboard while locked: set it from the service, check that a read returns nothing while locked, and look for access notices.
- A7. Receiving a file before and after the first unlock: a `directBootAware` service after a restart, then writes to app-private storage, MediaStore and a granted folder while locked.
- A8. Background killers on a Pixel, a Samsung, a Xiaomi and a OnePlus, for 24 and 72 hours, with default settings and then with the makers' recommended exemptions.
- A9. Keystore keys that need an unlocked device: encrypt while locked, confirm that decryption fails while locked and works after unlock, on Android 12, 13, 14 and 16 or 17 and with face unlock.
- A10. Notifications on the lock screen at each visibility level, and an action that requires authentication. Also a notification action and a Quick Settings tile that start a send, each with and without the authentication requirement (`setAuthenticationRequired`, `unlockAndRun`), used from the lock screen: record whether the code runs before the unlock, and whether the agent refuses the send while the phone is locked. Decides the sending rule in section 5.2.
- A11. Recovery after a kill while the phone is locked: with the foreground service running and the phone locked, stop it from the Task Manager, let a phone maker's background killer end it (a Samsung and a Xiaomi), and crash it. Record whether and when it comes back, with and without the battery-optimisation exemption and with a high-priority push. Settles the recovery rules in section 5.1.
- A12. An accessibility service as a way to lock the device: whether its lock action works from the background, what it is allowed to see of the lock screen, and what changes with Advanced Protection on (Android 17). Decides whether the accessibility route in section 5.6 is viable. Gestures over the lock screen are deliberately not tested, because Baton never types into a lock screen (V-A10 in the notes).
- A13. The reported automatic reboot after 72 hours locked: leave a phone with the relevant Play services version locked for 73 hours and record the reboot time, the state of the foreground service afterwards and whether the phone is in the before-first-unlock state (V-A14 in the notes).

**iOS and iPadOS**

- I1. Locked reachability: ping, UDP, Bonjour and a listener probed every 10 seconds for 30 minutes, plugged in and not, with and without cellular, noting when Wi-Fi drops.
- I2. Push paths: an alert push with `mutable-content` to the notification extension, locked after the first unlock and after a restart before it. Measure latency, a keychain read, writes in each file-protection class, a pasteboard write, and a request on the local network. Then background pushes at 1, 3 and 10 an hour.
- I3. Notification presentation with each preview setting, with Face ID on and off, and for actions with and without authentication, including whether an action handler runs while the device stays locked. Decides the sending rule in section 5.2 for notification actions.
- I4. ScreenCaptureKit on iOS across a lock, and whether App Store validation accepts the `screen-capture` background mode.
- I5. What an App Intent or a control may do while locked: a network request, a pasteboard write, a file write. Run it with the default `alwaysAllowed` policy and with `requiresLocalDeviceAuthentication`, and from a control, a widget button and Siri, and record whether a send can start before the unlock. Decides the sending rule in section 5.2.
- I6. AirPlay mirroring from the lock screen, and a Bluetooth keyboard or pointer at the lock screen.

**Mailbox and wake**

- D1. A prototype mailbox that stores ciphertext: a locked Linux source pushes an item, an Android phone in natural deep sleep is woken by a high-priority message and pulls it. Ten trials at 5 minutes, 1 hour and overnight, including after a reboot.
- D2. Wake-on-LAN sent from an Android phone to a sleeping wired laptop, with the local-network permission granted and denied.

## 9. What this does not cover

- Everything above comes from documents, source code and reports. No device was used.
- Not researched: other Linux compositors than Mutter and KWin (Sway, Hyprland, COSMIC), the suspend defaults of distributions, push without Google services, how GNOME Keyring and KWallet behave while locked, how the macOS login keychain behaves at a screen lock, KDE's lock-screen notification policy, Bluetooth-proximity locking, whether Android's device-admin lock is affected by its deprecations, and the default of Android's Low Power Standby.
- Not verified: TeamViewer and how AnyDesk and Parsec build their privacy modes; Dark Wake and Power Nap for third-party code on macOS; waking a Mac from the internet; the lock behaviour of iOS's newer screen-capture API; AirPlay from a locked iPhone; whether a sideloaded Windows package could run in Modern Standby.
- What would change these conclusions: a change to GNOME's policy on remote access while locked (for example an allow-list), KWin handling the lock in its screen capture, Google documenting how foreground services behave in Doze or changing Low Power Standby, the final behaviour of Android 17's local-network permission when it is denied, and any test result that contradicts an entry marked inferred.
