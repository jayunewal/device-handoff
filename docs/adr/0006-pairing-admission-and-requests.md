# 0006: Who is let in, and how requests are handled

Status: accepted

## Context

With the transport in place, anything on the internet can reach a Baton device and ask to pair, and paired devices can send items that end up opening links and changing the clipboard. Some choices here show on the wire, so they are expensive to change once other implementations exist. Others decide what a hostile peer can do.

## Decision

**Pairing is always listening, and treats everyone without a valid proof alike.** A device cannot know who will ask to pair, so it accepts `baton-pair/1` connections from anyone. A wrong proof, an expired or used offer, no offer at all, and a payload that cannot be decoded all get `Rejected(Invalid)` after the same 250 ms delay. Only someone holding the ticket's secret can learn anything else (`Busy`, `Declined`), and only a valid proof can make an approval prompt appear.

**A wrong proof does not spend the offer.** The secret has 256 bits, so guessing is not a threat. Counting wrong proofs, as the first draft of the protocol text did (three and the offer closes), would let anyone who knows the device id cancel every pairing as it starts. The device id is public: it is announced on the local network.

**A person approves every pairing, and compares a code to do it.** There is no always-yes approver outside the tests. The prompt shows the joiner's name, its key fingerprint and a six-digit code, and the joiner's own screen shows a code too; the person approves only if the two are the same. A proof tells the offerer that the joiner holds the ticket. It cannot tell the person whether that joiner is the device in their hand or a stranger who copied the ticket, because both prove it equally well, and a name or a fingerprint cannot either: a stranger chooses any name, and a fingerprint means nothing to someone who has not seen it before. The code is worked out from the ticket's secret, both device ids and the TLS session, and nothing about it is sent, so a stranger's request produces a different one. One offer is open at a time and works once, and it puts one request to the person. A valid request takes that place: from then on the offer's deadline does not take the question away, and it ends only when the person answers, when the approval time-out runs out, when the device that asked disconnects, or when the offerer withdraws the offer. All of those but the last spend the ticket. Declining ends the offer on purpose, since the person has just seen a request they did not expect and the ticket may have leaked. A joiner that disconnects while the question is on the screen is treated the same way, because the person never got to decline it and a stranger who copied the ticket could otherwise ask again and again, each time with a fresh code. A real joiner whose connection drops at that moment needs a new ticket, which is rare and cheap. A second holder of the ticket who shows a valid proof while the question is on the screen is answered `Busy`. That is the one place where a device learns that another holder of the ticket is being asked, so the joiner's message says so and tells its person to decline the request on the offering device and make a new ticket. Trying again with the same ticket cannot help, since every way the request can end spends the offer.

**An offer's deadline follows a monotonic clock.** The offerer decides whether an offer is still open by a clock that only moves forward, so setting the wall clock, by hand, by a network time service or by a daylight saving change, neither stretches an offer nor ends it early. The expiry time inside the ticket is only a hint. The joiner turns a ticket away only when it is more than ten minutes out of date by its own clock, so two devices whose clocks disagree by a few minutes can still pair. The deadline decides whether a request may be put to the person, not whether the person may finish answering it: a request that arrived before the deadline is answered after it, within the approval time-out, and nothing new is let in once the deadline has passed.

**The offerer stores the joiner before it answers.** If the answer is lost, the offerer trusts a device that does not trust it back, which is harmless and fixed by pairing again. The other order could leave the joiner trusting a device that never agreed.

**Trust is checked three times.** Right after the handshake (a stranger on `baton/1` is closed with 403 and leaves nothing behind), again for every request, looked up when the request arrives and not remembered from when the connection began, and when the trust store changes. A registry of open session connections, inbound and outbound, lets unpairing close them at once. The registry is filled from iroh's after-handshake hook, which runs for both directions, and only after the trust check passes, so strangers cannot make it grow. A connection is registered before the check is made a second time, so an unpairing that lands in between is seen by the check or by the registry, and a connection cannot slip through the gap.

**Floods are handled at the first packet.** An incoming attempt is judged by where it comes from before the handshake, so before any public-key cryptography is done or any state for the connection is kept. (By then noq has derived the Initial keys, which come from a public salt and the connection id, removed header protection and checked any retry token. That is cheap, and it is all.) While sources that have not proven themselves stay under 64 attempts a second they are let through, and above that they are asked for a QUIC retry token first, which a real client answers by itself. An address that has answered one is limited to 20 attempts per 10 seconds, and an IPv6 sender is counted by its /64, because a host can use any address in the /64 its network gives it and each address would otherwise be a new sender. Limiting an address before it has proven itself would let anyone lock out a real device by forging that address, which is why unproven sources are never rejected, only asked to prove themselves.

**Relayed senders are told apart by whether they are paired.** The relay has checked that the sender holds its key, but keys cost nothing: with a limit on each key, every attempt could use a new one and get a fresh allowance and a full handshake, and a sender that rotated through more keys than the table holds would make the limiter forget everyone. The endpoint id is known before the handshake, so it is looked up in the list of paired devices, the same list the gate uses afterwards. A paired device's key has an allowance of its own, 20 attempts per 10 seconds, which no stranger can use up. Every other key is treated like an address that has not proven itself: let through while few arrive, asked for a retry token under load (for a relayed sender that costs the client a round trip and a fresh ClientHello, and nothing more), and, once it has answered one, limited to 20 attempts per 10 seconds all together. Pairing over a relay goes through that shared allowance, which is enough for something that happens this rarely. Someone who keeps it empty can stop pairing over a relay for as long as they keep it up, and cannot touch paired devices. A paired device may hold 8 session connections.

**Idle strangers give way to newcomers.** At most 8 pairing connections are served at once, because anyone may open one. A table that turned the newcomer away when it was full would let eight idle connections shut out every real joiner for as long as they were held, and an address that stays inside the admission limits can open eight in a moment. So the newcomer takes the place of the oldest connection that has not shown a valid proof, and that connection is closed with 429. A connection has the request deadline, 10 seconds, to open its stream and send its request, and the deadline covers the wait for the stream because a stream with nothing written on it cannot be seen by the other side in QUIC: a deadline that started only once a stream existed would never start for a peer that does nothing. Keeping the table full of strangers then takes a steady stream of new connections, each with a finished handshake, and not eight idle sockets. A connection is protected from pushing out from the moment its request is about to be judged, because a valid one puts a prompt on the person's screen and that cannot be undone by stopping the connection halfway. It stays protected if the request was valid and becomes open to pushing out again if it was not, keeping its age. Only someone who holds the ticket can hold a protected place, and the offer allows one prompt, so this gives a stranger nothing to hold on to.

**Handoff is idempotent.** The sender picks an id and reuses it on retry. A receiver remembers the outcome per peer and id (1024 of them, for 10 minutes) and answers a repeat from memory, without charging it to the rate limit. Refusals that happen before the application sees the item (unsupported, too large, rate limited) are not remembered, so fixing the problem and sending again works.

**The core checks, the application decides.** `HandoffHandler` is called only with items that are well formed, from a paired device, of a kind it advertised, within the size and rate limits, and not repeats. What happens to an accepted item is its business.

**A failing handler cannot stop the node.** iroh's router stops accepting connections for good if any protocol handler panics. Every connection is therefore served in a task of its own, so a bug reachable from the network ends one connection and not the node.

**iroh stays behind `baton-core`.** Its types appear only in the networking modules (`node`, `gate`, `pairing`, `session`, `wire`, `link`), and in the public API only under the `test-support` feature. [ADR 0003](0003-iroh-transport.md) speaks of one module; in practice it is those six, and the boundary is the crate.

## Prior art and where this differs

This comes from reading public documentation of KDE Connect and RustDesk, not their source; their code is under licenses Baton cannot take from (see [ADR 0004](0004-apache-2-license.md)).

- Tools that let any device on the local network ask to pair, KDE Connect among them as I understand its documentation, trade some safety for convenience: whoever can reach you can make a prompt appear. Baton gives that up. A prompt needs the ticket, which is shown on a screen the person is looking at.
- The proof is bound to the TLS session and to both device ids with the TLS exporter, the same idea as channel binding in SCRAM-PLUS (RFC 9266 defines the TLS 1.3 form). A proof recorded on one connection is useless on another.
- Showing both people a number derived from the key exchange and asking them to compare it is how, as I understand the specification, Bluetooth's numeric comparison pairing works, and ZRTP's short authentication string follows the same idea. Baton's code also depends on a secret that only the ticket holder knows, so it appears only for a request that already passed that bar, and each offer allows one prompt, which is what makes six digits enough.
- Refusing everyone alike, with the same delay, is ordinary practice for login endpoints. Baton applies it to pairing, where a naive implementation would answer "no offer open" differently from "wrong code".
- QUIC specifies retry tokens (RFC 9000, section 8.1.2). Baton uses them only under load, so ordinary connections do not pay for a round trip.
- Delivery that is idempotent by id is a common technique. The detail Baton adds is that repeats are looked up before the rate limit, so a retry after a lost reply is never turned away for an item that was in fact taken.

## Alternatives considered

- **Show only the joiner's name and key fingerprint.** A stranger picks any name, and a fingerprint helps only someone who knows what to expect.
- **A short code the person types on the joiner.** A code short enough to type can be guessed offline from a recorded exchange, so it would need a password-authenticated key exchange and strict limits on attempts. It would be a different protocol, not a change to this one.
- **End the offer when a second holder shows a valid proof.** Anyone who copied the ticket could then cancel a real pairing at will. The second holder is answered `Busy` instead, and the joiner's message says what that means.
- **Warn on the prompt that a second holder exists.** It needs a new field on the approval request and a way to change a question that is already on the screen. The joiner's `Busy` message carries the warning for now, and needs neither.
- **Close the offer after a few wrong proofs.** Standard for short codes, harmful here for the reason above.
- **Refuse pairing connections when no offer is open.** Tells anyone who can reach the device whether it is offering to pair.
- **Turn newcomers away when all pairing places are taken.** Eight idle connections then keep every real joiner out for as long as the attacker likes. Pushing out the oldest stranger costs the attacker a steady rate of new connections instead.
- **Rate limit by address from the first packet.** Forged source addresses turn the limiter into a way to lock out real devices.
- **Limit each relayed key.** Keys are free, so a sender gets a new allowance by making a new key, and a flood of keys evicts the entries of everyone else.
- **Count IPv6 senders by their full address.** One host rotates through its /64 and each address gets a new allowance. A /56 would be stricter, since many networks hand out one to a household. It is a possible tightening; the cost is that devices at one home would share the 20 attempts, as devices behind one IPv4 address already do.
- **Check trust only when a connection is made.** An unpaired device would keep its access for as long as the connection lived.
- **Remember every item id forever.** Unbounded memory for a benefit that ends when the sender stops retrying.

## Consequences

- A flood can still make a device slow or unreachable. The limits make it harder, not impossible, and `docs/security.md` says so.
- A sender with a prefix larger than a /64 has one allowance for each /64 in it, and a distributed one has as many as it has addresses. These limits slow a flood down and do not stop one.
- A stranger who holds a leaked ticket has one chance in a million of matching the code by guessing, and only once: an offer puts one request to the person, and declining it, ignoring it or walking away from it all end the offer.
- A real joiner can still be pushed out by newcomers that arrive in the few milliseconds between its handshake and its request being read, which takes a rate of new connections far above what the admission limits let any one source start, and it only has to try again. Devices that are already paired are not affected: they use separate connections and limits.
- Rate limits and caches are in memory and start empty after a restart.
- Other implementations must answer pairing requests uniformly, or they leak whether an offer is open. The protocol document says so.
