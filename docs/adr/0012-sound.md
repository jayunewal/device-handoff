# 0012: Sound: the Mac sends what it plays, and is quiet while the other device listens

Status: proposed

## Context

[ADR 0009](0009-window-streaming.md) sends the picture of a Mac to a phone or a tablet and says that there is no audio in that version. [ADR 0011](0011-privacy-mode.md) adds that the speakers of a Mac that another device controls go on playing, which the people at home hear and the person at the other device does not. The person asked for the sound to travel the way the picture does: while a phone or an iPad is used on the Mac, what the Mac plays comes out of the phone or the iPad and not out of the Mac, as if an audio device had been attached to it.

What decides whether that can be built is how macOS lets a program take the sound of the Mac and keep it from the speakers. Nobody can read that off a build server, so it was measured first, on a Mac (macOS 27.0.1, a MacBook Pro with an M1 Pro), with a signed app bundle that started a tone program of its own and tapped that program. The check is called A1 here, and the program and what it found are in [docs/research/sound](../research/sound/README.md). What came out:

- A Core Audio process tap (macOS 14.2 and later, documented) hands a program the sound of the processes it names, or of every process but those it names, as samples, and has a mute mode in which what it reads does not reach the speakers.
- Every call succeeded. The tap gave 48 kHz, two channels, 32-bit floating point, in buffers of 512 frames (10.7 ms), without a gap in the sample times and at the level of the tone. The delay from the tone program rendering a click to the callback that held it was 21 to 25 ms at the median.
- With the mute mode set, the person heard nothing of the tone for as long as the tap read it, only a voice that the test played, and heard the tone again after the tap was destroyed.
- With the System Audio Recording permission missing, every call still succeeded and the tap delivered silence. No call tells the program that the permission is missing.
- A command-line tool got silence. The program has to be an app bundle with a usage text for audio capture.
- Not checked: a tap of every process but the app itself, which is what Baton needs; the sound coming back when the app is killed; any output other than the built-in speakers; whether a tap that lacks the permission still keeps the sound from the speakers; and the entitlement of a sandboxed app.

## Decision

**Sound goes with a whole screen, and with nothing smaller.** A source sends sound only for a session whose offer is of a `Display`. The sound of a Mac is the sound of every program on it, and the person who shares one window has not agreed to that. A tap of the programs of one window is no answer: a browser plays its sound from helpers that are not the program that owns the window. A session of a window has no sound, and the source answers a viewer that asks for it with a reason that says so.

**The viewer says when it wants to hear, and the source says what it can send.** Two new capabilities say who takes part: `stream.sound` is advertised by a source that can send the sound of its screen, and `stream.hear` by a viewer that can play it. A new kind of stream and new messages need a capability that the other side advertised first (protocol.md section 5), and a capability for each role keeps an older device from ever seeing one. Nothing in `Offer` or `Accepted` changes. The first version plays one codec, AAC-LC, so the capabilities name no codec, and a later codec comes with a capability of its own. After the viewer has taken the source's control stream it may send `Hear { on }`. The source answers a `Hear` that is on with `Sound`, the format of the packets that follow, or with `Silent` and the reason it cannot: the session is of a window, the Mac has not allowed the app to record its sound, or something failed. Sound goes in runs, from a `Sound` to a `Silent` or to a `Hear` that is off. All three are messages on the control streams and not requests. Whether sound is on at the start of a session is the choice of the application and not of the protocol.

**The Mac captures with a tap of everything but Baton, and is quiet while it does.** The tap excludes the app itself, so that a sound the app makes is not sent back. It is in the mute mode while the sound is being read, which keeps what it reads from the output (A1 heard this on the built-in speakers, and A2 checks headphones, Bluetooth and HDMI). The tap and its aggregate device are the app's: they go with the process, so a Mac whose app is quit, killed or crashed plays again (inferred, A2 checks it). A private session ([ADR 0011](0011-privacy-mode.md)) keeps the Mac quiet for its whole length, also while the viewer does not listen: the tap is made, muted, and what it reads is thrown away. Any other session mutes the Mac only while the viewer listens, and a session that is over, a viewer that has gone quiet for ten seconds (the rule under which the source stops encoding) and a viewer that has turned Sound off all leave the Mac playing. A missing permission is found out by listening: if the output of the Mac is running and the tap gives nothing but zeros for two seconds, the permission is missing, and the viewer is told with `Silent`. Whether the Mac stays audible meanwhile is for A3 to say, and the order of the steps (listen first without the mute, or mute at once) follows from it. The rule that does not wait for A3 is that a session which is not private never leaves the Mac muted for a viewer that hears nothing. The app asks the system for the permission when the person allows a device, where it asks for the screen, so that it is asked when someone is at the Mac to say yes. In a private session the Mac is dark and its keys are held, and nobody can answer the question there.

**The sound is AAC-LC in packets of 1024 samples, each on a stream of its own.** The system has an encoder and a decoder for it on a Mac and on a phone, so Baton adds no library. A packet is 21 ms of sound and about 340 bytes at 128 kbit/s. Each packet is one unidirectional stream of a new kind, 4, with a header of 34 bytes (the start, the number of the packet, when its sound was captured on the source's clock, and a flag for the first packet of a run) and then the access unit. The source numbers the packets and never sends one again. A session has one format of sound, so that a packet that comes late is decoded as the viewer knows to decode it, and a source that has to change what it captures converts the sound to the format of the session. The rules for the streams in flight follow those of frames in ADR 0009, with other numbers, which the tests settle and which are where the code starts: the source has 8 packets in flight (170 ms of sound), resets the oldest when it has one more to send than it may have in flight, since the oldest is the one nearest to being too late, and resets a packet that is not acknowledged within 200 ms and two round trips (1 s at most). The viewer counts the open streams of the kind, those in flight and those that were reset and are still counted, 16 at most, and stops one beyond the count with 499, as it does for frames. Sound goes at the priority of control streams, so it never waits behind a frame or a lane, and it takes a few hundred bytes a packet, so it holds back nothing. Packets are sent also while the Mac plays nothing: the sound of silence is a few bytes, and the viewer gets a steady clock to keep time by.

**The viewer puts the packets in order, with a short wait, and plays them through a buffer of its own.** Streams arrive in any order and QUIC repeats a lost packet after about a round trip, which on a home network is shorter than the buffer. So the viewer holds a packet that comes before the one it expects for a short time (40 ms to start with), delivers the packets in order as they complete, and calls what has not come by then lost, with a count, so that the player can cover the gap. A packet that comes after a later one was delivered is dropped as late. This is in `baton-stream` and has no clock but the one it is given. What the platform does is its own: it decodes the packets in order, resets the decoder at the first packet of a run, and writes the samples into a ring that the audio hardware reads. The ring starts playing when it holds about 80 ms and is kept between 40 ms and 250 ms: it plays silence and fills again when it runs dry, and drops the oldest sound when it is too full, which is also how the small difference between the clock of the Mac and the clock of the phone is absorbed. These numbers are starting values, and A4 and A5 settle them. Sound is not tied to the picture with a mechanism. The picture is shown as soon as it is decoded. The sound is the tap (about 25 ms), the encoder and the decoder (about 43 ms, two packets) and the buffer (80 ms) behind the Mac, which is about 150 ms (an estimate, A5 measures it). ADR 0009 aims at a picture no more than about 100 ms behind (R5), so the sound would lag the picture by about 50 ms, which is inside the 125 ms from which people notice that a voice and a mouth disagree (ITU-R BT.1359, reported). The capture time of each packet is kept for a correction and for measuring, which A5 shows whether it needs.

**Where the code lives.** The header and the messages are in `baton-stream`, with the rules for the packets in flight and for putting them in order, tested with a simulated link. The session (the new kind of stream, the count, the capabilities) is in `baton-core`. The interface to the platform stays at version 2 of the header, since a table is read as far as its size says: both tables get entries at the end, and a platform that was written before them is accepted as one without sound. The sharer gets `hear`, which says whether the other device wants to hear the sound of a display, and the library gets three functions with which the platform answers it: `baton_stream_source_sound_begin` says what the packets are, `baton_stream_source_sound_silent` says why there are none, and `baton_stream_source_sound` hands over one packet and is answered with a number and what became of it. Every `hear` that says 1 is answered with a begin or a silent, also by a platform that records already, since the other device starts a new run at each begin. The viewer gets `sound` (what the packets are), `play` (one packet, with its number and how many are missing before it) and `stopped` (why the run is over). The entries of a side come together or not at all, and a device advertises `stream.sound` or `stream.hear` only when its platform has them. Swift on the Mac does the tap and the encoding, Swift on the phone the decoding and the playing, and Dart the switch.

## How it will be measured

Without Apple hardware, on every pull request: the header and the messages have known encodings and are fuzzed; the rules for the packets in flight and for putting them in order are run against a simulated link with loss, reordering and a stall; the session is run between two nodes on loopback, with the count and the wrong cases (a packet beyond the count, a header that is wrong, a stranger, a session that is not running); the interface is run by a test in Rust that plays the platform; on a hosted Mac the encoder and the decoder are run on a tone, and the logic of the player is run on a computer without a Mac. A piece that only a Mac or a phone can check is never described as tested.

On a Mac and a phone, as in ADR 0009: record the builds, the models, the output of the Mac and the network. A1 is done.

- **A2. The tap of everything but Baton.** With the mute mode on, does what another program plays stay off the speakers, the headphones, a Bluetooth output and an HDMI output? Does the sound come back at once when the app is quit with Command-Q, killed with `kill -9`, or hangs? Is any sound heard in the first moments of a session, before the first read? Does it follow the volume key and a muted Mac, and a change of the output device or of its sample rate in the middle of a session? Does it go on while the Mac is locked?
- **A3. The permission.** The question the system asks, when, and whether the app has to be opened again after yes. What a tap does when the permission is refused (does the Mac stay audible?) and when it is taken away in the middle of a session. Whether two seconds of zeros while the output runs is a good test, and what a program that plays silence on purpose does to it. The entitlement that the sandboxed app needs, if any. Whether the system marks a program that records the sound of the Mac in the menu bar (reported).
- **A4. Quality.** Does a tone, music and speech sound right on the phone, with clicks and gaps counted over ten minutes on Wi-Fi, a cable and the relay, and with 1% and 5% loss? Does the buffer stay within its bounds over thirty minutes (the drift of the two clocks)?
- **A5. Delay and sync.** A clip with a click and a flash, filmed together with the phone's screen and its sound at 240 frames a second: the delay from the Mac to the ear, and the difference between sound and picture, on Wi-Fi, a cable and the relay. Decides whether the buffer or the codec changes, and whether the capture times are used to bring sound and picture together.
- **A6. The phone.** The silent switch, the volume buttons, AirPods and other outputs that come and go, a call or an alarm in the middle, the app in the background, the heat and the battery over thirty minutes.
- **A7. The private session.** With the Mac covered and held, nothing is heard at the Mac when other programs play, when an alert comes, and when the viewer turns Sound off, and everything is heard again after the lock.

## Alternatives considered

- **The audio of ScreenCaptureKit.** The capture of a display can include its sound, and it has no way to keep the sound from the speakers (reported), so the Mac would play and the phone would play too.
- **A virtual audio device.** An audio server plug-in that the Mac plays to, which Baton reads, would silence the speakers too. It has to be copied into a system folder by an administrator, cannot live inside a sandboxed app, and changes the settings of the Mac. It is the way to the other direction, from a phone to a Mac, which this record leaves alone.
- **Datagrams.** They fit sound better than frames, but the setting is fixed in the handshake and is off for the pairing connections for the reasons of ADR 0009. A packet on a stream of its own that is reset when it is late has the same effect, with the repeat of a lost packet for free while it is still of use.
- **One stream for all the sound.** It saves a stream a packet and gives order for free, but it cannot let go of the old without resetting the new that is behind it, so after a stall the sound that was late would arrive before the sound that is not.
- **Raw samples.** No codec, no priming and no cookie, at 1.5 Mbit/s for 48 kHz in two channels, which is much more than the sound needs and a large part of what a slow link has to spare. The codec field leaves room for it.
- **Opus, or AAC with a lower delay.** They would take 30 to 50 ms off the delay. Opus is not documented for the system's converter as well as AAC is, and the low-delay forms of AAC need a description of the stream that the decoder reads before the first packet. Both fit the codec field with a capability of their own, and A5 says whether they are worth it.
- **A codec list in `Accepted`.** It would let a viewer name the codecs it plays for each session. The rule of protocol.md section 5 asks for a capability before a new kind of stream is used, a capability says the same thing for the whole connection, and a change to `Accepted` would touch what the other work on that message is changing.
- **Sound of a window.** See the first decision.
- **Mute the Mac by its volume.** It changes a setting of the person and has to be put back, which a crash does not do.

## Consequences

- A viewer of a screen can hear everything that the Mac plays: the sound of messages, a call, a video. It already sees the screen. Sound is on only when the viewer turns it on, except in a private session, where the application turns it on at the start. The permission of the Mac for it is separate from the one for the screen.
- A viewer that is not allowed to control the Mac can still turn the sound on and so make the Mac quiet. That changes nothing but the output, and it is undone when the session ends.
- The Mac is quiet exactly while its sound is read, or for the whole of a private session. A Mac whose app hangs with the tap in place stays quiet until the app is ended.
- A private session on a Mac that lacks the permission is not quiet, and whether that should refuse the session, as a missing consent for the keys does in ADR 0011, is open. Until it is decided it does not, and the app tells the person at the Mac, where they allow a device, that the permission is missing.
- Sound keeps on while a session is paused for a locked Mac, if the tap goes on reading while the Mac is locked (inferred, A2 looks).
- The line of ADR 0011 that says that sound is not part of the promise is superseded by this record once the Mac side is built.
- The app needs the usage text for audio capture, and, for the sandbox, one more entitlement if A3 shows that it is needed. The guard of ADR 0011 does not tap, since a program that is not an app bundle gets silence, and a Mac whose app died plays again.
- A path that stops costs the sound of the stall. When it comes back, the packets that the source kept are the newest eight, up to about 0.2 s old, and they arrive together; the player has to drop the sound that is older than its buffer allows, or the sound stays that much behind. A path that carries only half of the sound plays about half of it, in pieces, and keeps within about 0.3 s of the Mac. Both are what the simulated link shows with the starting values of the rules, and the player and A4 decide what to do about them. The simulated link does not hold a place at the viewer for each stream that the source reset, as the session does, so it may show the first more kindly than a real path will: A4 measures it.
- The rules are made for a path whose round trip is under about 170 ms, which is the sound of the 8 packets in flight. Above that, a packet is still unacknowledged when the one that is 8 packets after it is to be sent, so the source resets every packet before its acknowledgement can come back, though the packets arrive. The viewer counts a stream that was reset until the reset is acknowledged, one round trip later, so a stream holds its place for 170 ms and a round trip, and 16 places carry all of the sound only up to a round trip of 170 ms. Above it the source skips the packets that find no place, though the path could have carried them: by this count about 3% of the sound at 90 ms each way, 8% at 100 ms and 28% at 150 ms, and a simulation of the rules found 6%, 11% and 30%. A home network is far below that round trip. A path above it wants a rule that resets the oldest packet only once it is older than a round trip, and a count of places to match, and this record leaves that until a path of that kind can be measured.
- The things that cost the phone something (the decoder, the ring, the audio session) are small, and A6 reads them.
- There is no sound from a phone to a Mac here, and no microphone and no camera.
