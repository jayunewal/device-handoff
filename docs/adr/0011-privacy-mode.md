# 0011: Privacy mode: a Mac that another device controls shows nothing, takes no input, and locks when the session ends

Status: proposed

## Context

[ADR 0010](0010-sessions-that-the-viewer-starts.md) lets a phone or a tablet start a session on the person's own Mac and control it. The Mac is awake and unlocked while it is used so, and it is used while other people are at home. Three things follow that the person does not want:

- The screens of the Mac light up and show what the person does, and the pointer moves by itself.
- Anyone who walks up to the Mac can read it and use it, since it is unlocked.
- When the session ends, or the connection drops, the Mac is left unlocked, and so it is if the app dies.

The person asked for these, in this order of importance. While they work from another device the Mac must not light up or show their activity. If the Mac was unlocked from afar, nobody else may use it. Ending the session, or losing the connection, locks it again, and a Mac that stays unlocked is a security failure. Looking at the Mac in the picture of the other device must stay as it is.

None of what the design depends on was known for macOS 27, so it was measured first, on a Mac, with a sandboxed program that has the limits of the app (docs/research/privacy-mode). What came out:

- An active tap on the keys and the pointer can be made inside the sandbox, drops what it is told to, and lets through what the app itself posts, since the window server names the process that posted an event.
- A program that has the keyboard to itself, such as a password field, blinds a tap to keys. The time since the last key press, which the system keeps, still moves, so a key that nobody posted can be found.
- A cover made of windows hides the banners, the menu bar and the Dock, and not Spotlight or Mission Control. Setting the table of every display to nought makes every display black whatever a window shows, hides those too, and the capture does not see it.
- The Mac can lock itself from inside the sandbox, with a call of the system, with the shortcut for the lock posted as keys, and with the sleep of the display on a Mac that asks for its password at once. The lock was seen by the session within a third of a second each way.
- A sandboxed helper that the system keeps alive can be registered by the app, can see the same consents, restarts about half a second after it is killed, and finds the flag of a session that its predecessor left. That is a means to lock a Mac whose app died, and the guard below does not use it (see the alternatives).

## Decision

**A session is private when a paired device started it and has control.** In that case, and only then, the app asks the platform for the screen as `Private`. A session that only looks is not private, since it moves nothing at the Mac, and a session that the person started at the Mac is not, since the person is there. A look-only session can be made private by the same means later, without changing the interface.

**The platform promises, from the start of the session until it is over, three things.** The screens of the Mac are dark. The keys and the pointer of the Mac are out of anyone's hands but the session's. When the session is over, whatever the cause, the Mac is locked again, and only then do the screens and the keys come back. A platform that cannot promise this refuses the screen. It never gives the screen open instead, so a Mac that lacks a consent, or lacks the means, starts nothing, and the device that asked is told why. The interface of the platform changes for this (`BATON_STREAM_ABI_VERSION` 2): `screen` takes flags, with `BATON_SCREEN_PRIVATE`, and can answer that the app has not been let hold the keys and the pointer. A platform of version 1 is refused at registration, since it would be called with arguments that it does not read.

**The cover is the table of each display.** The output of every display that shows something is set to nought, black whatever a window shows, and set again four times a second, which covers a display that is plugged in or wakes (it can light for up to a quarter second). A display that does not take the table, twice in a row, ends the session. A display that is asleep shows nothing and is left alone. The capture is of the screen as it is, so the other device sees the Mac as before. No window is used for the cover, since the windows of the system show over it.

**The hold is a tap that drops everything the app did not post.** The tap is active, at the lowest level that the system allows, on a thread of its own, and it drops the events of the keys, the pointer, the wheel, a tablet and the gestures of a trackpad, except those that the window server says this process posted. Three presses of Escape in two seconds at the Mac end the session and lock the Mac; they are the way out of a Mac that shows nothing and answers nothing, and the presses that the other device sends do not count. A tap that the system switches off is switched on again. A tap that cannot be kept on ends the session. The tripwire for what the tap cannot see reads, four times a second, how long ago the system counted the last key press: a key that went down after the hold began and after the last key that the app posted ends the session, and the Mac locks. It sees the last key only, so a key at the Mac that is followed by a key from the other device before the next look is not seen.

**The Mac is locked by the first of three ways that works, and the cover stays until it is.** The call of the system that locks the screen, then the shortcut for the lock posted as keys, then the sleep of the display. Each way is given two seconds, and the next is tried when the session does not say that the screen is locked. The cover and the hold come down a second after the session says so, so that the lock screen is drawn first. A Mac that cannot be locked keeps its cover and its hold and goes on trying, since a screen that shows the desktop is the way of failing that the person said is not acceptable. The person at the Mac has the power button. A quit of the app (Command-Q, its menu, closing its window), which the person at the other device can cause by mistake, is heard before the process ends, and it is the end of the session like any other: the Mac is locked and the cover comes down before the quit goes on. The quit waits for that for ten seconds at most, and goes on after them whether or not the Mac is locked; the guard locks it then.

**A guard locks the Mac if the app goes away.** The app does not end every way: a crash, a kill, and a hang give the screens back as the process ends or stops looking after them, and nothing in the app can lock the Mac then. For each private session the app starts a small program of its own, `BatonGuard`, from inside the app, and talks to it over the pipes of its standard input and output in short lines: it says `arm` and the guard answers `ok`, then `beat` four times a second, while the cover goes up (for ten seconds at most, so that a cover that hangs is not told well) and then while the cover and the hold are in place and still looked at, and at the end `done`, which the guard answers and ends on without locking anything. The guard is armed before the cover goes up and let go of after the cover is down, so nothing is ever covered that nothing would lock. An armed guard locks the Mac, with the same three ways in the same order as the app, and ends, when its input closes (the system closes the app's end of the pipe when the app's process ends, however it ends), when no line comes for three seconds (the app hangs, or is stopped, or nothing looks after the curtain any more), when it is told to terminate, or when the process that started it is another one. A guard that is not armed ends when its input closes, or after ten seconds, and locks nothing. The guard is signed to inherit the sandbox of the app, which is the only way it runs, and the app is refused a private session when the guard cannot be started or does not answer: the device that asked is told why, and nothing is covered. A guard that goes away, or locks the Mac, while the session is on ends the session, with a sentence, and the Mac locks.

**The person at the other device can lift the cover and the hold, each by itself and as often as they like.** A private session starts with both in place. In the settings of the session, on the phone or the tablet, there are two switches: one lets the screens of the Mac show what is on them, for a person who wants to show the Mac to somebody, and one lets the keys and the pointer of the Mac be used at it, for a person who wants to sit at it. Each message of the viewer (`Lift`) says the state of both, so the last one is how things stand, a message that changes nothing is not passed on, and one that is lost is made good by the next. Only a session whose offer set `private` takes it, and a source offers `private` only when its platform can lift what keeps the screen private (the table of the sharer grows by one function, `lift`, at its end; a table without it is accepted, keeps the screen private all the same, and does not tell the other device that it may lift anything). A session always starts with both off, and nothing is remembered for a device: whatever the last session ended with, the next one begins dark and held. The platform makes the change on the thread that looks after the cover and the hold, so that no look is half way through it. Screens that are lifted are not covered and not looked at. The tap stays on while the keys are lifted and lets everything through, so that holding again needs no new tap and a tap that is lost is still found; the tripwire and the three presses of Escape are off for as long as the keys are lifted, since nobody is suspected of a key that they were let press. What is put back goes in the order of the start, the hold first and then the cover, so that nobody can type or click while the screens are dark. Nothing that is lifted outlasts the session. Whatever ends it (the other device, the connection, an alarm), the platform puts everything back and then locks the Mac, and a part that cannot be put back ends the session, so the Mac locks all the same. Lifting does not touch the guard: the app goes on looking after the curtain and the guard goes on hearing that the session is well, so an app that dies with something lifted has its Mac locked like any other.

**The device that asked is told what ended the session.** A session that something at the Mac ended ends with a sentence that says what, and that the Mac locks, in the same way as a capture that stopped. A session that the other device ended, or that dropped, ends as it does, and the Mac locks all the same.

**The consents are those of the hold.** The app needs Accessibility and Input Monitoring. The platform does not rely on the list of consents; it makes the tap, and lets go of it, when it is asked for a private screen, and the system's answer to that is the answer. When the tap cannot be made, the platform asks the system for the consents once in each run of the app, opens the list that lacks the app, and answers `NeedsInputPermission`, which the device that asked is told as a sentence about Accessibility and Input Monitoring. Asking when the person saves the setting for a device, as for recording the screen, is for a later change.

## Alternatives considered

- **A cover made of windows.** It is cheaper to build, and Spotlight and Mission Control show over it. A table that is set to nought has neither fault.
- **The lock screen as the cover.** It hides everything, and a locked Mac takes no clicks, so the other device could do nothing, and it lights the screens. Unlocking from afar is a later record.
- **Let the displays sleep.** A display that sleeps is not captured, and the session would end.
- **Turn the backlight to nought.** The brightness of the built-in display could not be read from the sandbox, so none could be set and put back, and the person judged the Mac dark enough with the table alone.
- **A helper outside the sandbox to hold the keys.** The tap works inside the sandbox, so none is needed for this.
- **A launch agent that the system keeps alive for the guard.** It was shown to work, and the system restarts it when it is killed. It needs the person to approve it in System Settings, a build that is signed with an identity that stays the same for the approval to hold (the Mac builds are signed ad hoc, and each build would ask again), and a way to tell a helper left over from an older build from the current one. A program that the app starts for each session needs none of that, and exists only while a session does. What it gives up is the restart: a guard that is killed alone is not started again, so the app ends the session and locks the Mac when it learns that.
- **Lock when the session has been idle for a while.** A Mac that is covered and held shows nothing and takes nothing, and a lock that the other device cannot undo would end every long reading of a page. It can be added with a setting.
- **A switch in the setting for a device, set before the session.** A show of the Mac begins and ends in the middle of the work, and the person asked to switch it as they go, any number of times in one session. A setting would need the session to end first.
- **Remember the switches for a device.** A device that remembered would begin its next session with the Mac lit, or with its keys open, because of how the last one ended or a tap by mistake. The person chose that every session starts dark and held.
- **One switch for both.** The two are different needs with different risks: lit screens show what is typed to whoever is near, and open keys let them use a Mac that is unlocked. The person asked for each to be configurable.
- **A message for each switch.** A message that says "on" or "off" for one part leaves the state at the Mac to the order in which the messages arrive, and a lost one makes the two sides differ for the rest of the session. A message that says the whole state has neither fault.
- **Hide the pointer too.** The table hides it at the Mac. Hiding it with the call that exists could take it out of the picture of the other device, and what the picture shows of the pointer was not settled by the checks.

## Consequences

- The app needs one consent more than for control: Input Monitoring. Accessibility is the list that control already needs. A build that is signed ad hoc may lose them with each build, as it loses the consent to post input events. Without them a start from a phone is refused with a sentence, and the Mac does nothing.
- **An app that dies during a private session (a crash, a kill) leaves the Mac lit for as long as the guard takes to notice, and then it is locked.** The system puts the tables back when the process ends, and the tap goes with it, so the screens show the desktop until the guard has locked the Mac: the closed pipe is seen within a fraction of a second, and the lock takes the time of the first way that works (each way was seen within a third of a second). After a hang the screens stay dark for the three seconds that the guard waits, and the keys may come back for a moment, since a process that does not answer probably loses its hold of them (inferred, not tried); the lock follows after the three seconds. A hung app that is still alive probably goes on holding the tables of the screens, so they stay dark after the lock until it is ended; that was not tried. Those are the windows in which the screens show the desktop or the keys work, and they are the reason that the guard watches the pipe and not only the clock.
- **The guard has limits.** Killing the app and the guard together (`pkill -f Baton` matches both) leaves the Mac open, since nothing else is watching. A guard that is killed alone is not started again, and the session ends. The guard is a program of the same app, signed the same way, so a person who can kill the app can kill it. The shortcut for the lock needs the consent of the app to post keys, and a guard that has not got it locks the Mac with the other two ways.
- **That a program which inherits the sandbox can be started by the app and outlives it was not part of the checks that came before this record.** The first run of this change on a Mac is that check, and the steps in the pull request say what to look for. A build whose guard does not start refuses the private session with a sentence and does nothing else.
- A key that is typed at the Mac while another program has the keyboard to itself (a password field, a terminal with secure keyboard entry) reaches that program before the tripwire sees it, which can be a character or two. The session then ends and the Mac locks. A key at the Mac that is followed by a key from the other device before the next look is not seen at all, so while the other device goes on typing into such a program a person at the Mac can put more than that there. The count of key presses of the system has no such gap, and whether it goes up for a key that the tap was blind to, and for the keys that this app posts, was not measured; the next checks on a Mac are to read it.
- The gestures of a trackpad that the system handles without events, such as the swipe between spaces, are not held. They act on a Mac that shows nothing, and cannot be seen. The power button, Siri and Voice Control, if the person turned them on, are not held either.
- Whether a display that shows HDR content takes the table was not tried, and a display that is lit at nought still glows a little. The Touch Bar of an older MacBook Pro is not one of the displays whose table is set. A program that writes the tables of the displays itself, such as one that tints or dims the screen, lights them until the next look each time it writes; the read-back right after the set does not see it.
- **Sound is not part of the promise.** The speakers of the Mac go on playing what the session starts (a video, the sounds of alerts and messages, a call), which the people at the Mac hear and the other device does not, since the stream carries no sound. Muting the output of the Mac for the session and putting it back after the lock is a candidate for a later change; it was not tried from the sandbox.
- A Mac that locks itself during the session, or that the other device locks, stays covered until the session ends, or shows its lock screen if the screens are lifted. The other device cannot unlock it; that is the next record.
- A Mac that stays covered because it cannot be locked is a Mac that its person cannot use until they press the power button. It is the failure that was chosen over the one in which it shows the desktop.
- **While the screens are lifted, everyone near the Mac sees what the session does, what is typed and the notifications included. While the keys and the pointer are lifted, anyone at the Mac can use it, unlocked, and nothing at the Mac ends the session**, since the tripwire and the three presses of Escape are off. The person at the other device decides both, and puts them back with the same switches or by ending the session, which locks the Mac. A person who forgets that a switch is on leaves the Mac as it is until they end the session.
- **A device that vanishes while something is lifted leaves the Mac as it is until the session ends.** A connection that closes ends it at once. A device that stays connected and stops reporting ends it 30 seconds after its last report, at the latest (protocol, section 9). The Mac is locked then, and everything that was lifted is put back first.
- A key or a button that is down at the Mac when the keys are held again may stay down as far as the system knows until it is next pressed or clicked, since the hold drops the release and the system does not see that it was dropped. The keys that went down while the Mac was open are not the tripwire's to judge, and the count for it begins again at the hold.
- A display that does not take the table when the screens are put back, three tries in a row, ends the session and the Mac locks. The tables are read back right after they are set, so a program that writes them itself can still light a display until the next look.
- A platform that has no `lift` (an older build) keeps a private screen private for the whole session and does not offer the switches, and an older viewer that ignores `private` shows the session all the same, with no switches. Neither can lift anything, which is the side that fails safe.
- Looking and controlling are separate in the setting for a device (ADR 0010), and privacy follows control. A device that is allowed only to look sees the Mac as it is, and the people at the Mac see it too.
- The rules (when the cover goes up and comes down, what ends a session, how the Mac is locked until it says so, the counters) are Swift that builds and is tested on any computer with a compiler. What the Mac does is `BatonPrivacyMac.swift`, built on a Mac and checked on one by the steps in the pull request.
- The work is split so that each pull request can be read on its own: the first (the interface, the reason, the cover, the hold, the lock when the session ends), the second (the guard, which is the one that adds the pipe protocol, `BatonGuard.swift`, `BatonLock.swift` and the program in `apps/baton/macos/Guard`), then the two switches that lift the cover and the hold, which are for a person who wants to show the Mac to somebody or to sit at it.
- The rules of the guard (when it locks and when it ends, the lines, the case of a parent that is killed) are Swift that builds and is tested on any computer with a compiler, with real processes that are killed and stopped. How the Mac is locked is `BatonLock.swift`, which the app and the guard share, so that the two lock it in the same way.
