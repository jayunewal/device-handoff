# 0020: Screen Sharing at the login window: Baton speaks Apple's own screen sharing instead of capturing the screen itself

Status: proposed, and already revised once before anything was built. The first shape of this record had the phone speak Apple's protocol directly, carried between it and the Mac opaquely, through a Baton session of its own. Reading Baton's own networking code (`baton-app`'s `Starts`, `baton-core`'s `streaming`) found that a Baton session is built to send pictures, frame by frame, with its own codec, clock and congestion rules, not to carry another protocol's bytes untouched; nothing in it hands back a plain pipe, so carrying Apple's protocol as first written would have needed a change to the code every Baton feature shares, not a small addition. The shape below asks nothing of that code: the Mac-side program speaks Apple's protocol itself, as a client of it, and feeds what it gets into the same picture-streaming every other Baton session already uses, so the phone needs no change at all for this. The person separately reported, on his own Mac, Screen Sharing reachable and a sign-in going through with it after a restart (2026-11-12); which exact moment that was tried at, against the login window itself and before any sign-in, is not yet confirmed apart from his word for it. Nothing below is built.

## Context

[ADR 0019](0019-baton-at-the-login-window.md) chose a small Baton of its own, a program that would capture the login window's screen and post the phone's taps and keys into it. Its own two unproven points, L1 (events) and L2 (capture), were tried on the person's Mac (macOS 27.2, 2026-11-11) and both failed: the window system answered that the program "is not permitted to connect to or launch Window Server before login", and posting input needs a private entitlement that nothing the person can sign with carries. ADR 0019's own "If the two unproven points fail" named what would be left if this happened: "the plug-in of the authorisation, which ADR 0013 turned down for what a faulty one does... and which gets a record of its own before anyone builds it." This is not that record, because the plug-in did not reach the point of needing one either.

An authorization plug-in (a `.bundle` in `/Library/Security/SecurityAgentPlugins`, loaded into the chain of mechanisms that macOS runs for the `system.login.console` right) was the person's next choice. A throwaway plug-in was built and tried in a lab virtual machine, never on the person's own Mac, with three shapes: putting a name and a password into the chain's shared context before the stock mechanism that shows the window runs, doing that and then asking the engine to deactivate the mechanisms after it and start again (`RequestInterrupt`), and replacing the stock mechanism outright with one that only sets the context. None of the three signed the account in without typing; the window this project is trying to get past stayed up and waited every time. Something in Apple's chain checks a password where it is not reading the values that these mechanisms set, and no public source read for this project says what. The plug-in also only ran inside the login window's own process because the lab machine's own protections are looser than a normal Mac's; getting it to run there on a real Mac logged a rejection from the operating system's library-validation check (AMFI), which exists to keep exactly this kind of code out of exactly that process, since that process is also where a password is typed. Working around that check is not attempted here, whether or not it would have helped: the sign-in did not work even where the plug-in loaded.

Four ways were found and put to the person on 2026-11-11: carrying Apple's own screen sharing to the login window, the plug-in above, turning FileVault off (which the person's own rule refuses, since it gives up the disk's protection), and signing in by hand, with no remote part at all. The other ways that ADR 0019 had already ruled out for itself (an authenticated restart, which only covers a restart that the Mac starts and still stops at the login window; device management, for which no command that signs a person in was found; and the Mac App Store, closed to a sandboxed app that must write outside its sandbox) are ruled out here for the same reasons. The person chose Apple's own screen sharing.

What is known about it, and how well:

- Apple's Screen Sharing, turned on in System Settings under General and Sharing, runs a VNC server. Two people who posted about it online report that it was unavailable at the full login window on a FileVault Mac until someone signed in, on recent macOS; one of the two says turning FileVault off fixed it. Neither post is about the exact macOS version or Mac in front of this project, and neither was checked here. Against that, ADR 0019's own Context has the person's phone reaching the Mac's SSH door, over the network, while the Mac sat at that same login window for about three minutes after a restart (observed once, 2026-11-11): the network was up there, at least for that one Mac on that one try, and at least for port 22. The person later reported trying Screen Sharing itself after a restart of his own Mac and reaching it, with a sign-in going through once he typed the account's password into it (2026-11-12); he did not separately say that the screen he saw was the login window itself, before any sign-in, as against some other moment of the restart, so V1 and V2 still check it directly rather than resting on his word alone.
- Screen Sharing, on a Mac that is already running, can ask for either of two things before it shows anything: the name and password of an account on the Mac that System Settings allows to connect, or a separate VNC password kept in its own settings and good for any viewer. The person's report above, that typing the account's own password signed the account in, suggests it was the first of the two at the login window as well, but nothing read for this project confirms that apart from the one report, and V2 looks directly.
- Screen Sharing, once turned on, listens for a connection on port 5900. Reported, not checked here: it listens on every network interface unless something on the Mac stops it, which would let any device on the same network as the Mac try to reach it, not only a device that goes through Baton.

## Decision

**Baton's Mac-side program speaks Apple's protocol, as a client of it; the phone speaks none of it.** A program of its own (keeping the name `baton-loginwindow`) runs before anyone signs in. It opens the plain connection to `127.0.0.1:5900`, where Apple's own screen sharing listens, the moment a paired phone asks to start a session — the same `Start` [ADR 0010](0010-sessions-that-the-viewer-starts.md) already decided, answered the same way every other screen session on this Mac is. From there it is a client of Apple's protocol: what that protocol sends of the screen it turns into a picture for Baton's own session to share, the way a captured picture would be, and what the phone does on that session (its taps, its keys) it turns into the pointer and key messages the protocol expects, sent back over the same connection. The phone's own screen for this looks like any other Baton session, because it is one: nothing new is built there, and nothing of Apple's protocol ever reaches it.

**This is possible, where ADR 0019's capture-and-post program was not, because reading Screen Sharing asks nothing of Window Server.** ADR 0019's program needed Window Server itself, before anyone had signed in, to capture a picture and to post events into the window underneath, and that is exactly what was refused (L1, L2). Speaking Screen Sharing's own protocol over a plain network connection to `127.0.0.1` asks nothing of Window Server: it is ordinary network code, of the kind the rest of Baton already is, talking to a service that macOS itself already runs before anyone signs in, on the understanding that the network itself is reachable then, which is what V1 checks.

**It may not need to run as root, and nothing ties it to the login window's own session.** ADR 0019's program had to be an agent for the `LoginWindow` session type, because only that gave it the window-server session it needed to capture and post into, and running that way is why it had to run as root: launchd ignores a dedicated user (`UserName`) for an agent, honouring it only for a daemon. This program touches no window server, so nothing holds it to a session type at all. V3 below tries it as a plain launch daemon, loaded at boot the way any other daemon is, under a user made for it and nothing else — a stronger claim than ADR 0019 could make for its own program, and more than the first shape of this record could do more than hope for.

**Not being bound to a session's own end means the program has to tell the login window apart from a signed-in account itself, with a call of its own, and does.** ADR 0019's agent would have answered a `Start` only while it existed, and the system itself ended it at sign-in; a daemon that is loaded at boot is not ended that way; nothing stops it running, and answering, long after someone has signed in. So before it answers a `Start`, the program asks macOS directly whether anyone is signed in to the Mac's own console session — `CGSessionCopyCurrentDictionary`, a call that is public, needs no entitlement and touches no window server, the way `kCGSessionLoginDoneKey` in what it returns is read today by other software to tell a locked or signed-out Mac from one that is not — and refuses the `Start` if the answer says someone is. This is also what covers the gap a later review of the first shape of this record found: a login window that macOS shows again over an account that stayed signed in, when a Mac with several accounts is asked to switch to another one. Nothing ends that first account's own session when its login window merely stops being the one on the screen, so a check that only asked "does the login window still exist" would have missed it; asking the console session directly does not. V8 below tries it.

**The device and the pairing are ADR 0019's, unchanged.** Its own key, its own list of paired devices in a folder that only its owner can read, pairing done in Terminal at the Mac with the person typing the six digits that the phone shows, printing nothing else that a device sent with its question and not its name, ending the trust of a phone only with `baton-loginwindow forget` or the remove script, run at a Mac that someone has signed in to, and installed by the person with an administrator's password as plain files, checked against the same certificate as the app. None of that depended on capturing a screen, or on carrying a protocol opaquely, so none of it needs to change for either. [ADR 0019](0019-baton-at-the-login-window.md)'s Consequences about this shape (a program that answers a stranger before anyone signs in, and what a flaw in that code would give up) apply again here and are not repeated.

**Screen Sharing is kept from being reached except through this program.** Turning it on opens a door on the Mac that was not open before, the same cost ADR 0019 already named for this alternative when it considered it. Apple's own Application Firewall, in System Settings, allows or refuses a program and says nothing about a port or an address, so it cannot do this; `pf`, the packet filter macOS also ships with no System Settings page of its own, can, with one rule for IPv4 that refuses an incoming connection to port 5900 from anywhere but the Mac itself (`127.0.0.1`) and one for IPv6 that does the same for its own loopback address (`::1`), loaded at boot so that it is in place before the login window that needs it is. The program above, which dials that address from the Mac's own side, still reaches it; a device on the same network that tries the Mac's address directly gets nothing; and a device that reaches Baton's own program still has to pair with it first, the same door every other part of Baton has. Whether the rule can be loaded before the first restart that needs it and survives one, and whether Screen Sharing still answers over loopback once it is in place, is V4. The install script adds the rule and turns Screen Sharing on; the remove script takes the rule back out and turns Screen Sharing off again too, or tells the person to, since it was this program that had a reason to turn it on in the first place.

**The phone's own rule, checking the person at it before any screen starts, needs nothing new here.** This session is, to the phone, the same kind as any other ("Checking the person at a phone before it asks for a screen" in [security.md](../security.md)), so the rule that already runs before every `Start` runs before this one too, with nothing of its own to add or to explain: the phone does not know, and does not need to know, that what it is shown came from Screen Sharing rather than from a capture.

**What this session does not have, until it ends.** The program has no window server either, the same as before: it cannot cover this Mac's own display or hold its keyboard locally, the way the private session of a signed-in Mac can ([ADR 0011](0011-privacy-mode.md)), so a session shared this way has none of Baton's own rules once it is open: not the dark screen and held keyboard, not the guard that locks the Mac if the app dies, not the limit to one shared window. ADR 0019 already judged this acceptable for the login window itself, for the same reason this record relies on again: nothing private is shown there, since nobody has signed in yet, so there is nothing for a cover to protect. That reasoning stops being true the instant the account signs in, which is also the instant this session would be looking at a real desktop with none of Baton's safeguards around it, so the program closes the connection to `127.0.0.1:5900` itself the moment it sees the login window close, rather than leaving that to the person or to a later change, and refuses a new `Start` from then on for as long as the console session check above says someone is signed in. Closing the connection at the sign-in shortens the gap; it does not close it. Closing it for good is still the later change ADR 0019 named: a program that takes the Mac over, dark and held, from the sign-in until the phone's own session starts.

**Nothing is built before V1 and V2 answer, in their own right and not only on the person's word.** ADR 0019's own observation (port 22, once) and the person's later report together point toward a network that is up and a Screen Sharing that answers at the login window on this Mac, against two online reports that say the opposite happens on others; V1 and V2 check both directly, rather than resting on reports that disagree, or on a try whose exact moment was not pinned down. If they find that nothing answers, or that only port 22 ever does, this record is replaced the way ADR 0019's capture-and-post idea was.

## Alternatives considered

- **Baton carrying Apple's protocol between the phone and the Mac, opaque to Baton.** The first shape of this record, before anything was built. The phone would have needed a client of a protocol Baton did not write, and the Mac-side program would have needed to hand it a plain pipe instead of a picture, which nothing in Baton's own networking does today: a Baton session sends pictures, frame by frame, with its own codec, clock and congestion rules, not arbitrary bytes of another protocol, so this would have changed code that every Baton feature shares rather than added to this one. Replaced by the shape above once that was found, before any of it was built.
- **Baton's own capture-and-post program**, ADR 0019 as first written. L1 and L2 failed on the person's Mac.
- **An authorization plug-in.** A spike found that none of three ways made the account sign in without typing, described under Context, and taking it further would mean working around a protection of the operating system that is there to keep code out of exactly the process this record also has to get code into. Not built, and not reconsidered unless V1 and V2 both fail and this whole record is replaced.
- **Turning FileVault off, with automatic sign-in.** Gives up the disk's own protection, which the person's rule keeps.
- **Signing in by hand.** No remote part at all, and the fallback until the rest of this record is built or found not to work.
- **Apple Remote Desktop, in place of a client this project writes.** It is a paid product built on the same Screen Sharing service as the rest of this record, so it answers the same V1 and V2 as a free VNC client already does, and nothing it adds reaches further before sign-in. Not worth its cost for what this record needs.
- **A client of Baton's own streaming protocol instead of Apple's, reached by a capture-and-post program.** Would keep every one of Baton's own rules through the sign-in, with no gap at all, but needs the capture that the record before this one found could not be built. Not possible until it is.

## Consequences

- **A client of a protocol Baton did not write now runs on the Mac, not the phone.** It reads what Apple's own, already-running Screen Sharing sends over a connection to `127.0.0.1` that nothing but this program on this Mac ever makes, so a bug in reading it is reached only by what that local service sends, not by an arbitrary remote peer's own crafted bytes the way a flaw in code that answers a stranger over the network would be. The same care the rest of Baton takes with what a peer sends (see "Strict message decoding" in [security.md](../security.md)) still applies to it, since what is read came from outside this program all the same.
- **Screen Sharing is one more thing listening on the Mac.** It did not run before, and this record turns it on and keeps it answering only `127.0.0.1` and `::1`, not any address that another device on the network could reach, with a firewall rule for both. Nothing in the system holds that rule in place once it is wrong or once a later change on the Mac removes it, the same caveat ADR 0019 made about the code that answers a stranger: the list here is what the install script sets out to do, not a limit that something else enforces.
- **The program's own privilege no longer rests on a session type, and its own check of who is signed in takes the place that a session's end would have.** Not needing Window Server means V3 can try the plain daemon that ADR 0019's program could not be, under a user of its own rather than root; it also means nothing stops that daemon running, and answering, after someone has signed in, unless the `CGSessionCopyCurrentDictionary` check named above is built and correct. A flaw in that one check is now what a flaw in the session-bound agent's own end would have been for ADR 0019: the one thing standing between "nobody is signed in" and "a stranger who passed the phone's own check reaches a real, uncovered desktop."
- **The gap after sign-in, which ADR 0019 already named, is reached through a session with none of Baton's own cover, hold or relock**, for whatever time passes between the account signing in and the program closing the connection (V6 measures it). ADR 0019 left its own fix, a program that takes the Mac over dark and held from the sign-in to the phone's next session, for a later change; it is still that.
- **Everything ADR 0019 said about the device and the pairing applies again**, since the shape is reused: a program that runs on the network before anyone signs in, what a flaw in the code that answers a stranger would give up, and a lost phone removed only with `baton-loginwindow forget` or the remove script, at a Mac that someone has signed in to.
- **Every macOS release can change what Screen Sharing allows at the login window, and what `CGSessionCopyCurrentDictionary` reports.** V1, V2 and V8 are checked again after an upgrade, the way ADR 0019 said L1 and L2 would be.
- **If V1 or V2 fails, signing in by hand is what is left**, until this record is replaced the way ADR 0019's was.

## How it will be measured

On the person's own Mac, since nothing here can be tried without Apple hardware:

- **V1. The network at the full login window, again, and pinned to that exact screen.** Restart the Mac, unlock it with the phone over SSH as before, and once it is sitting at the ordinary login window (account tiles or a name field, not the disk password screen) and before anything else is done, from another device check whether anything answers at its address at all: port 22, which ADR 0019's own observation already found open at this same screen once, and port 5900, which the person's own report (Context) suggests but does not, by itself, pin to this exact moment. If neither answers here, that observation does not hold generally and nothing below can be reached; this record is then replaced.
- **V2. Screen Sharing answers there, and asks for what the person's report suggests.** With Screen Sharing turned on beforehand and V1 showing that something answers, does port 5900 take a connection at that same screen, does it show the real login window, and does it ask for anything of its own first or go straight to it? Does typing the account's own password there sign the account in, the way the person's report (not yet pinned to this exact screen) said it did elsewhere?
- **V3. The program's own privilege, and its own check of who is signed in.** Can `baton-loginwindow`, built as a plain launch daemon under a user made for it, reach `127.0.0.1:5900` and listen on Baton's own network before anyone has signed in, the way launchd starts other daemons at boot? Once a session exists, does `CGSessionCopyCurrentDictionary` correctly say so (`kCGSessionLoginDoneKey`), both at the ordinary login window (should say no) and once the account is signed in (should say yes)?
- **V4. Kept off the network.** With the firewall rules in place, does a port scan of 5900 from another device on the same network get nothing over IPv4 or IPv6, while the Mac's own program still reaches it over `127.0.0.1`? Does the rule take effect before the first login window that needs it, and does it survive a restart?
- **V5. Signing in through it.** Does typing the account's password through the phone's session sign the account in, the way typing at the Mac's own keyboard would? How does it look and feel next to one of Baton's own ordinary sessions: the delay, the picture, whether the person can tell the two apart at all, which the Decision means to avoid needing.
- **V6. The gap at sign-in.** From the moment the account signs in to the moment the program closes the connection to `127.0.0.1:5900`, how long, measured the way L6 of ADR 0019 measured its own gap? Does the person see anything of the signed-in desktop in that session before it closes?
- **V7. Away from home.** V1 and V2 again, reached through the route of [ADR 0017](0017-away-from-home.md) instead of the home network, since reaching this from away is the whole point of building it.
- **V8. A switch of user.** With one account signed in, switch to the login window that a switch of user shows (the same check ADR 0019's own L9 named), and send a `Start` from a phone paired with this program. Is it refused, the way the Decision's console-session check says it should be, even though the login window on the screen is, for this check, not the one for the account that is still signed in underneath?
