# 0008: File transfer: one offer, lanes of their own, hashing while sending

Status: accepted

## Context

Handing over a link or some text takes one small request. Handing over a photo library, a build folder or a 4 GB video is a different job, and it is where a person feels the speed of a tool. The roadmap sets two targets for it, stated against the raw throughput of the link so that they mean the same on any network: one large file at 90% or more of it (about 100 MB/s on gigabit Ethernet), and 10,000 files of 10 KiB in no more than twice the time of one file of the same total size. There is no compatibility with another tool's protocol, so the wire format can be shaped for those targets.

Four things decide whether a transfer reaches them, and each has a trap that a design written without them walks into:

- **One stream is a window per round trip.** ADR 0007 sized the QUIC windows so that one stream can carry about 73 MB/s at a round trip of 100 ms, and two streams about 147 MB/s. A design that sends a file on one stream leaves the rest of a faster long path unused.
- **A wait per file is a wait per round trip.** On Wi-Fi a round trip is a few milliseconds. A protocol that waits for an answer after each of 10,000 files spends 20 seconds waiting at 2 ms, against the one second that 100 MB takes at 100 MB/s. Small files are fast only if nothing waits between them.
- **Control and bulk traffic share one connection.** The session protocol allows 16 concurrent streams and times a stream out after 10 seconds without progress (protocol.md section 3). Those numbers suit requests that are small. A transfer that counted against them would be cut off by the stall timer, would crowd out a ping or a handoff, or would need the 16 raised for every peer.
- **The receiver is often a phone.** An iPhone or iPad runs Baton only while it is on the screen and has little memory to spare, so it can receive several gigabytes only if nothing is held whole. Memory has to stay bounded by the windows, not by the size of what is sent.

The standing rules from earlier decisions apply: a transfer is a request from a paired device, so it passes the same checks as a handoff before anything is written; the person decides what a device may put on their disk; and nothing here is opened or run (security.md).

## Decision

**One offer, decided once.** A transfer is a set of files and folders sent as a whole. The sender makes one `Offer` request that carries a random transfer id, a summary (number of files, folders and bytes), a preview of the top-level names, and the BLAKE3 hash of the full list of files. The sender walks the whole tree before it offers, to count the files and bytes, so the list exists and hashing it costs almost nothing. The receiver answers once: accepted, by its policy for that device or by the person, or declined with a reason. A person is given two minutes, as in pairing, and the sender waits for the answer on the same stream, with a deadline of its own: a request waits 30 seconds for its reply, which is not enough here, and the joining side of pairing has a longer deadline for the same reason. Everything after a yes is the enforcement of what was agreed, never a second question. The list that follows on lane 0 must hash to the value in the offer, which makes "must match" exact, names and sizes included, and it must also agree with the summary and the preview, which are what the person saw. A list that does not is a protocol violation.

The sender repeats the same offer when it retries, as with handoffs, and a repeat has to be the same offer, not only the same id. For a transfer, unlike a handoff, data follows the decision, so a yes for three files and 2 MB must not be reusable under the same id for three thousand files and 20 GB. The receiver remembers its decision for the whole offer: the id, the summary, the preview and the list hash. It answers a repeat from memory, without asking the person again, only if all four are the same, and it declines any other offer under a known id as `Invalid`. It keeps the decision for as long as it keeps the staging folder, which is longer than the ten minutes for which it remembers a handoff id, so that a transfer that resumes later is not put to the person again.

A whole transfer counts as one item against the device's rate limit and as one entry in the inbox, whatever it holds. Files have limits of their own: a number of transfers active at once, a quota of bytes, and a reserve of free disk space that a transfer may not use.

**Data moves on lanes: unidirectional streams, with a budget of their own.** After a yes the sender opens up to four unidirectional QUIC streams to the receiver and writes the data on them. QUIC limits bidirectional and unidirectional streams separately, and the receiver sets both limits. Requests keep the 16 bidirectional streams and the stall timer of section 3. Lanes get their own budget of unidirectional streams, enforced by the QUIC layer, so that a transfer cannot take the stream of a request, or a request the place of a lane. The budget is eight on a connection: four lanes for each of the two transfers that one device may have active at once. A stranger is given none: the limit starts at zero, as it does now, and the gate raises it for a paired device at the same moment and by the same hook as the connection window (ADR 0007). The call exists on the connection (`set_max_concurrent_uni_streams`, iroh 1.3.0, `endpoint/connection.rs`). The unidirectional limit does not change what a peer can make a device hold: the connection window is still the bound, and lanes only share it.

The budget is per connection, and a device may keep eight connections to one peer (protocol.md section 6), so "two transfers at once" is a count that Baton keeps itself, for the peer and across its connections. An offer beyond it is declined with `RateLimited`. Every lane starts with the id of its transfer and its own number. A lane for a transfer that was not accepted, or one with a number beyond the lanes that the receiver granted, is stopped, and the receiver checks that the peer is still paired when it takes a lane, as section 3 asks it to for each request.

Lanes are sent at a lower priority than request streams (`SendStream::set_priority`, noq 1.3.0), so data that is buffered for a lane never goes out before data that is buffered for a request, such as a ping or a handoff. That is all that priority gives. Flow-control credit and the send window are taken when data is written, first come first served (`write_limit` in noq-proto's `streams/state.rs`), so lanes that keep writing keep the credit used up, and a request that is written during a transfer waits for the next credit update, just as a lane does. The receiver sends one after it has read an eighth of the connection window, 2 MiB. That is milliseconds on a fast path, about two seconds on a relayed path at 1 MB/s, and as long as the disk takes to write 2 MiB on a receiver whose disk is slower than the link. So a transfer does not starve a request, which has streams of its own and its place in the queue, but it can delay one by about one credit update. The benchmark measures that delay, as the time of a ping while a transfer runs, in all four cases. If it turns out to matter, the next step is to put the lanes on a second connection with an ALPN of its own, on which the gate raises the stream budget and the window, so that requests keep a window that lanes cannot use. That is a change to the sender and the gate, and this record would then be amended.

A receiver that wants no more data stops a lane with a stop code (`STOP_SENDING`), and the sender sees that its write failed, with the code. The receiver sends nothing back along a lane, so there are no acknowledgements to wait for or to count: QUIC already delivers the bytes reliably and in order within a lane.

**The list of files goes first, the data shares the lanes, and nothing waits per file.** The first frames on lane 0 are the full list of files: relative paths, sizes and modification times. Then every lane carries spans. A file is cut into spans of 1 MiB (the last one shorter), and a span is a small binary header followed by the raw bytes, with no CBOR around the data. Spans of one large file can go on different lanes, which is how a long path gets more than one window; many small files go back to back on one lane, each as a single span. There is no answer per file and no answer per span. The receiver writes each span at its offset in a file whose size it knows from the list.

The list must not get stuck behind the spans. The receiver needs the whole list before it writes anything, because it checks the sizes, the quota and the free space first, so until the list is in it leaves the spans on the other lanes unread. Flow-control credit is taken when data is written, and lanes 1 to 3 can hold up to 24 MiB between them, more than the 16 MiB connection window. If the sender wrote spans while it was still writing the list, the spans could take the whole window. The rest of the list could then not be sent, the receiver would not read the spans before it had the list, and nothing would move until the 30-second timer ended the transfer. A list takes on the order of a hundred bytes a file, so a large folder is enough. Two rules prevent it. The sender writes the whole list to lane 0 before it writes a span to any lane, so that the list's bytes have their credit before any span takes some (a packet that is lost is sent again without new credit). And the receiver reads lane 0 to the end of the list before it reads spans from the other lanes. The list is also the one thing that the receiver must hold whole before it writes, so it has a cap of its own, in entries and in bytes (protocol.md section 6): 100,000 files, 100,000 folders, and 16 MiB for the list in the form in which it is hashed, which is the size of the connection window. A sender whose list is larger offers it as more than one transfer.

**The hash is made while sending, in any order.** Each file has a BLAKE3 hash. BLAKE3 hashes a file as a tree of 1 KiB chunks, and a span of 1 MiB that starts on a 1 MiB boundary is a complete subtree of it. The `blake3` crate exposes the chaining value of such a subtree (`hazmat::HasherExt::set_input_offset` and `finalize_non_root`) and the merge of subtrees into a parent or the root (`merge_subtrees_non_root`, `merge_subtrees_root`). So either side can hash the spans in whatever order they come, at the speed of the lanes and in step with the disk, and merge the values at the end into the same value that `b3sum` prints for the whole file. A file of one span or less, the empty file included, is hashed as a root, so that the value of its only span is the hash of the file; the non-root form is not the hash of a file, and it panics on an empty input. The tests include files of 0, 1, 1024, 1 MiB and 1 MiB + 1 bytes. The sender hashes what it reads and sends the file's hash after the last span has been read. The receiver merges its own values and compares. No pass over the data is made before the offer, so the first byte leaves at once, and none is made afterwards. A file whose hash does not match is not put in place.

**Nothing appears until it is complete, and nothing is replaced.** The receiver writes into a staging folder that it makes on the same volume as the destination, and moves the items into place once the transfer has ended and the files in them have been checked, leaving out any file that did not check out. A person never finds a folder that is still being received. Nothing is replaced either, and that needs a move that refuses to replace. `std::fs::rename` replaces a file that exists, and an empty folder too (on a Mac, on Linux, and on Windows 10 and later with NTFS), so looking for a free name and then renaming would lose a file when two transfers finish together with the same name: both find it free, and the second rename replaces the first file. The receiver uses `renamex_np` with `RENAME_EXCL` on a Mac, iPhone and iPad, `renameat2` with `RENAME_NOREPLACE` on Linux and Android (made through `syscall`, so that the C library does not have to be a recent one; everything named here is in the `libc` crate), and `MoveFileExW` without `MOVEFILE_REPLACE_EXISTING` on Windows. When the name is taken, it takes the next number and tries again. On a volume that does not support the call, the item fails and is named in the result; it is never moved over another. These are system calls, which need unsafe code that Baton forbids everywhere else, so they live in a crate of their own, `baton-fs`, whose tests run on Linux, macOS and Windows.

**Names are the receiver's to choose, and they are untrusted.** The receiver chooses the name of every entry, not only of the top-level items, and compares names the way its file system does: not case-sensitively where the file system is not, and after Unicode normalisation where the file system does it. A name that would take the name of an item that already exists gets a number before its extension, and so does a name that would clash with another in the same folder of the same transfer. An iPhone's file system tells upper and lower case apart and a Mac's, by default, does not, so a folder from an iPhone can hold `Notes.txt` and `notes.txt`, and the second becomes `notes 2.txt`. The staging files are made with `create_new` and the staging folders with `create_dir`, never `create_dir_all`, so a clash that slips through never mixes the bytes of two files or the contents of two folders: a file whose name is taken fails alone, and a folder whose name is taken stops the transfer before anything is written. A path that is both a file and a folder (`a` and `a/b`) cannot be in an accepted list, because every entry must be inside a folder that was listed before it. A path that is absolute, that has a `..` or an empty part, or that is too long or too deep makes the whole transfer invalid. Characters that a file system on the receiving side does not allow (control characters, the reserved Windows names, trailing dots and spaces) are replaced, and so are the characters that only disguise a name, such as the text-direction controls that make `invoice`, U+202E and `fdp.app` look like a PDF. The preview is text that another device wrote, and it is what the person decides on, so it is shown under the same rules as a device name (`sanitize_display_name`, protocol.md section 3). Links, devices and other special files are not sent, and a link inside a chosen folder is not followed, so that it cannot send what it points to (`~/.ssh`, say). Permission bits are not carried (a received file is never executable), and where the system has a mark for files that came from elsewhere, as a Mac does, received files get it, and on a Mac so do folders, since an app is a folder and Gatekeeper reads the mark of the app's folder and not of the files in it. The counts and sizes of the list are checked against the summary, the quota and the free space before the first byte is written.

**Progress is judged over time, and the end is told.** A transfer that has moved no byte for 30 seconds is stopped, and a long one is not stopped for being long. The sender ends a transfer with a `Finish` request after the last lane is finished. The receiver answers it when every file has been checked and put in place, with the result (done, or which files failed and why), so "sent" is shown only when the other device has it. The answer waits for the checks and the moves, so `Finish` has a deadline of its own, as the offer does, and a sender that gives up waiting sends it again and gets the same outcome. `Done` says that every file that is not named as failed has been checked against its hash and is in place under its final name. It does not say that the disk has stored it. Flushing each of 10,000 small files would cost more than moving them, so the receiver does not wait for the disk per file, and a power cut just after `Done` can lose files that the sender has shown as sent. What the sender's screen says is that the other device has received them, which is what is known.

**A transfer outlives a connection, and a file that changes is caught.** If the connection drops, the sender reconnects and offers again with the same offer. It keeps the list and does not make a new one, so the repeat is the same offer and the list hash is the same. The receiver, which has kept what it received for as long as it keeps its decision, answers with the spans it already has, and the sender sends only the rest. The answer names the spans and carries no hashes: the sender reads those spans again and hashes them, without sending them, so that the hash it sends for a file is always that of the bytes as they are now, and never a merge of values kept from before the drop with values of what it reads now. A file that was edited in between then fails its check, because the spans that the receiver kept are of the old version and the hash is of the new one. That and the next check are the whole protection against a file that changes while it is being sent. The hash cannot see such a change, because both sides hash the bytes that the sender read. So the sender compares the size and the modification time of each file with the list before its first read, after its last read, and before it resumes the file, and a difference fails the file. This catches what programs normally do, which changes the modification time or the size, and it does not see a change that keeps both. A file that fails (it changed while it was being sent, it did not verify) fails alone and is named in the result; the other files are kept. A resume is the usual path for a large transfer to a phone and not a corner case: the screen is kept on only while pairing, so Auto-Lock can stop a long transfer partway.

**Everything that decides speed is a local choice, and the benchmark settles it.** The wire carries a transfer, its list and its spans, and the number of lanes the receiver allows. It does not carry the size of a read, the size of the windows, the congestion controller or how many files are read at once: each device chooses its own, and they can change without a new protocol. ADR 0007 left three questions to this record: the stream window for long transfers, the congestion controller (Cubic, which is the default, or BBRv3, which the QUIC library ships) and behaviour on a path with loss. They are answered by measurements, below, and not here.

The wire format, the limits and the rules of the receiver are specified in protocol.md section 8. The capability that guards all of it is `transfer.files`.

```
sender                                                   receiver
  | -- Offer {id, summary, preview, list hash} ------>   |  request stream, may wait for the person
  | <--------------------- Accepted {lanes: 4} -------   |
  | == lane 0: list of files, then spans, hashes ====>   |  unidirectional streams, lower priority
  | == lanes 1 to 3: spans, hashes ==================>   |
  | -- Finish {id, files, bytes} -------------------->   |  request stream, answered when all is in place
  | <---------------------------------- Done {..} ---   |
```

## How it will be measured

A benchmark that anyone can rerun is part of the work, with the four cases of the roadmap, each aimed at a different limit: one large file, thousands of small files, Wi-Fi under load, and a relayed connection. It is a command (`baton bench`) that can make the data itself, so that the network and the protocol are measured without a disk, and that can also send a real folder, which brings the disk in. Every run also measures the raw rate of the link with a plain TCP stream between the same two addresses, and reports Baton's rate as a share of it, so a result means the same on a laptop pair and on a phone.

- **Large file and many small files** are run on a real link, between two machines, and on an emulated path on one machine (the harness of ADR 0007, which can add delay and a rate, and a pair of virtual network devices with `netem` for loss). The two targets are tested on the real link.
- **Wi-Fi under load and relayed** have no target until there are measurements. They are reported with their loss, round trip and rate.
- **Requests during a transfer.** Every case also reports the time of a ping while the transfer runs, next to the time of one on an idle connection, so that the wait for a credit update, described above, is measured and not guessed.
- **The settings to compare** are the number of lanes (1, 2, 4, 8), the congestion controller (Cubic, BBRv3), the stream window (8 and 16 MiB) and the number of files read and written at once. Rates are reported for long runs and apart from slow start, as ADR 0007 did, and with the spread of at least five runs.
- **Where the ceiling may be the CPU, not the network.** Baton builds iroh without its fast Apple datapath, which calls private system functions that the App Store rejects, so a Mac or an iPhone probably sends one packet per system call. That should be enough for gigabit and may not be for 10 Gbit/s. The benchmark reports processor use for each run so that a limit set by the CPU is not mistaken for one set by the network. If it turns out to be the limit, more than one QUIC connection for a big transfer is the next thing to try, and is a change to the sender alone.

## Alternatives considered

- **iroh-blobs.** It is a content-addressed transfer on iroh, but a transfer starts with a hash of the whole content, which is a pass over a large file before the first byte moves, its own documentation calls this version not yet production quality, and it brings a second protocol with its own rules for who may fetch what into a design where the paired device is the only authority. Hashing while sending gives the same integrity without the pass.
- **A stream for each file, on the request streams.** Simple, and no state to keep, but the 16-stream budget would limit a transfer to 16 files in flight, a stream's setup would be paid for each of 10,000 files, and a transfer would be cut off by the timer meant for requests.
- **Bidirectional lanes with acknowledgements.** The receiver could report progress and errors on the same stream as the data. It would take streams from the 16 that requests have, and an acknowledgement per span adds traffic that QUIC's own delivery makes unnecessary. A stop code and a final `Finish` carry what is needed.
- **Lanes on a second connection with an ALPN of its own.** It would keep requests out of the lanes' credit, so that a request never waits for a credit update that lanes are holding up, and it would give the transfer a congestion controller of its own. It costs one of the eight connections a device may keep, a second handshake and a second gate, and the benchmark has to show that the wait for a credit update matters before it is worth that. It is the next step if the measured wait is too long.
- **A second protocol on its own port, such as HTTP over TLS.** It is a common choice for local transfer tools. It cannot use the relay or hole punching Baton already has, needs its own way of knowing who is calling, and would be a second thing to keep in step with the first.
- **A hash of the content of every file before the offer.** It would put hashes in the list and let the receiver skip what it already has. It costs a full read of everything before the first byte moves, which on a large folder is more time than the transfer. Skipping what is already there is a sync feature and is left for later. The list hash in the offer is a different thing: it is made from the names, sizes and times that the sender has from walking the tree, and reads no file.
- **Hashes made in order.** Simpler, but then every file would have to travel on one lane, in order, and a large file could use only one window.
- **Compression.** Photos, video, archives and most documents are compressed already, and compressing costs a phone's battery. A capability can add it per span later for text-like content if a measurement shows it pays.
- **Rsync-style differences, and the receiver pulling files.** Both are real needs and neither is part of the first transfer. Pulling is planned in the planned extensions of protocol.md (section 10) and would reuse the lanes.

## Prior art and where this differs

Cutting a file into pieces that are hashed on their own is how BitTorrent works, and the `blake3` crate documents its tree interface for building such protocols. Several streams or connections for one transfer are how download managers and parallel SFTP clients get past a window. Writing to a temporary name and renaming when finished is common practice. This record does not claim to have invented any of them.

What it adds, as far as I know, and I have not searched exhaustively:

- **A stream budget for bulk data that is granted after the trust check and enforced by QUIC.** The same idea as the window raise of ADR 0007, applied to the number of streams: a stranger can open none, a paired device a small fixed number, and the QUIC layer counts them. Baton counts only transfers.
- **One decision for a whole transfer, and a staging folder that makes it atomic.** The person is asked once, and what they accepted appears whole or not at all.
- **One pushed offer that pins the whole list, and the standard hash as the result.** The `blake3` documentation offers its tree functions for exactly this kind of protocol, which the paragraph above credits, so the hashing is not new. What may be new is its combination with an offer that carries the hash of the list, so that "the list must match" is exact, and with a file hash that is the standard BLAKE3 value, so a person can check it with `b3sum`.

## Consequences

- The transport settings change in one place: the unidirectional stream limit is zero until the gate raises it for a paired device, and a test shows both halves, as it does for the window. The compile-time relations of ADR 0007 hold unchanged, because lanes share the connection window.
- protocol.md section 3 used to say that peers do not open unidirectional streams. It now says that a peer that is paired may open them for the lanes of an accepted transfer, and no others, and that a stranger may open none.
- A paired device can make another hold at most what ADR 0007 says, and can have two offers waiting for a person at the same time at most, counted for the peer across its connections. Further offers are declined with `RateLimited`, so a peer cannot use up the 16 request streams with questions.
- Disk is the new resource a peer can use. It is bounded by what the person accepted, the quota of bytes, the reserve of free space, and a limit on the size of what is staged. The list, which the receiver holds whole, has a cap of its own. Staging folders that are not resumed are removed.
- The moves into place need system calls that Baton's rule against unsafe code forbids elsewhere, and so do the mark on a received file, hiding the staging folder on Windows, and the question of how much room a volume has. They are kept in `baton-fs`, a crate that has nothing else in it, and tested on Linux, macOS and Windows.
- A transfer needs both devices to be awake and connected. A phone that leaves Baton or locks stops receiving; the sender keeps trying for a while, and the transfer resumes when Baton is on the screen again. Whether the screen should stay on while a transfer runs is a decision about the product, made separately.
- An iPhone or iPad keeps what it receives in the app's own folder, which the Files app can show, and a Mac in the Downloads folder. Those choices, the picker and the screens belong to the app and are described with it.
- A device that sends files must be told that the other one can receive them, by the capability, before it offers. An older device is never sent an offer it cannot read.
- The benchmark can be repeated, and its numbers go into this record when they exist. Until then the two targets are goals and not results.
