# 0009: Window streaming: the Mac offers, late frames are dropped, input stays in the window

Status: proposed

## Context

Handing over a link or a file moves something once. Streaming a window moves a live picture, and it is where a person feels whether Baton is a remote desktop or a slideshow. The goal, as the person put it: sit at a Mac, pick up an iPhone or an iPad, and carry on in the same window from there, with touch and keyboard. The roadmap puts it after file transfer (milestone 5), with a Mac as the source and an iPhone or iPad as the viewer. Other sources and viewers follow the same design later.

It is written down before it is built because the facts that decide it are behaviours of Apple's frameworks that nobody can watch from a build server. Continuous integration has no screen, no permission dialogs and possibly no hardware video encoder. So this record keeps apart what the documentation says from what has to be tried on a Mac and a phone, and it orders the work so that most of it can be built and tested without either. Claims about platforms carry the labels of locked-devices.md section 2: documented (official documentation, which here includes Apple's sessions, or a formal specification), reported (source code or a credible report), inferred (reasoned from other facts) and unknown (no evidence either way).

Five things decide whether it works, and each has a trap that a design written without it walks into:

- **Late is worse than lost.** A picture that is correct and a second old cannot be used to point or to type. A reliable, ordered transport turns a slow link into lag and not into a coarser picture. The delay is the sum of three queues: pictures waiting for the encoder, frames waiting for the network in the sender's buffer, and frames waiting for the decoder and the screen. A design that reacts only to loss lets all three grow. A picture has to be dropped before it is queued, and a frame that is late has to be abandoned and replaced, not delivered.
- **Video frames depend on each other.** In the encoder's low-latency mode every frame after the first key frame is a P frame, with no frame reordering and no periodic key frame (documented, in the reference for the low-latency specification of VideoToolbox). Abandoning one frame makes every later frame of the run undecodable until the viewer is given something it can decode from: a key frame (large, for a screen full of text) or a frame that refers only to a frame the viewer is known to hold. So dropping is not a local decision, and the sender has to know what the viewer holds.
- **One connection carries everything.** Requests (16 bidirectional streams and a stall timer of 10 seconds), the lanes of ADR 0008, and now video and control share one QUIC connection, one congestion controller and one connection window. Priorities order what waits in the sender and do nothing about queues in the network. A file transfer on the same connection can delay a frame by a credit update (ADR 0008), and Cubic, the default controller of this version of the QUIC library, fills queues until it sees a loss, which on a home router can mean a delay of hundreds of milliseconds or more (inferred).
- **Input crosses a trust boundary.** A picture shows what the person chose to show. Input lets a phone act as the person at the Mac. A synthetic click goes to whatever is under the pointer when it arrives, and the picture the phone tapped on is some frames old, so "click where the phone tapped" and "click in the window that was shared" are different things.
- **The platform decides, and part of it is not written down.** Screen Recording consent, whether the system's picker avoids it, whether a sandboxed app may post input events, and whether a hosted Mac in continuous integration has an encoder at all are not answered by the documentation. Baton is installed from Xcode with a free Apple ID, so nothing here may depend on an entitlement that Apple grants by application, and the one for persistent screen capture is such an entitlement (documented).

The standing rules from earlier decisions apply. The source is unlocked while it streams, with a visible notice, and Baton never unlocks anything and never touches the lock screen (locked-devices.md section 5.8). A stream is a request from a paired device, so it passes the same checks as a handoff before anything is shown. The person decides what a device may do on their computer, and decides it at that computer.

## Decision

**The Mac offers, once for each session, and the phone answers.** A session starts at the source. The person picks what to share in the system's own picker (`SCContentSharingPicker`, macOS 14 and later, documented), so that the choice is made in a dialog that the system draws and not in a list that Baton draws, and picks a paired device to show it to. Baton sends one `Stream` request, an `Offer`, with a random session id, the kind of source (a window, or a whole display), its title and the name of its app, its size in pixels, and whether control is granted (below). The viewer answers once: accepted, by its policy for that device or by its person, or declined with a reason. The default policy is to ask, and a person is given two minutes, as in pairing. An accepted answer carries what the viewer can take: the largest picture, the highest frame rate and the codecs it decodes. The title and the name are text that another device wrote, so they are shown under the rules for device names (`sanitize_display_name`, protocol.md section 3) and are never written to a log. A session counts as one item against the device's rate limit whatever it carries, and a peer can have one offer waiting for a person at a time, counted across its connections as in ADR 0008. The capabilities are `stream.share` (a device that can offer a window), `stream.view` (a device that can show one) and `stream.control` (a device that takes input from a viewer), and a device does not send a kind of request that the other did not advertise.

A viewer cannot start a session on a Mac in this version. That is the pull case among the planned extensions of protocol.md, and it is left out on purpose. It would let a paired phone make a Mac start capturing its screen, which is a different grant from showing a window to a phone that the person is holding, and it is of no use when the Mac is locked. It can be added later as a policy that is off by default.

**What the person agreed to stays visible, and control is a separate choice made at the Mac.** When the person makes the offer they choose between "view only", which is the default, and "view and control". That choice is the consent: a viewer cannot ask for control, and a phone cannot turn it on later. The person at the Mac can turn control off at any time, and a session that began as view only becomes a controlled one only by a new offer. For as long as a session runs the Mac shows which device is viewing, what is shared, whether that device can control it, and a Stop button. The phone shows that it is viewing or controlling, and which Mac. Unpairing a device closes its connections (protocol.md section 3), which ends its sessions, and either side can end one with a `Stream` request, a `Stop`. The first time a person shares, Baton asks macOS for Screen Recording and says in its own words what it is for and where to switch it on if it was refused. macOS may need the app to be started again after the person allows it (reported).

**The Mac stays unlocked and awake while it streams.** Baton holds a power assertion that stops idle display sleep for as long as a session runs and lets it go when the session ends. It does not stop the person from locking the Mac. When the Mac locks, or its display goes off, nothing documented lets Baton capture it, so the session is paused, the phone says that the Mac is locked, and nothing is shown or typed. When the person unlocks, the session goes on; a session that has been paused for ten minutes ends. This is what locked-devices.md section 5.8 requires of the first releases, and the same page records that no documented API captures a locked session. Tests R10 below and M8 there could change it. The same assertion keeps the Mac from locking itself when it is idle (inferred: the automatic lock follows the screen saver and the display sleep, and R10 checks it) for as long as the session runs, while the person is, by design, away from the Mac. So the notice on the Mac says that it stays awake and unlocked until the session ends, and a session whose phone has gone ends by the rule for liveness below and lets the assertion go. Locking the Mac when a session ends is the optional step of section 5.6, and the hand-over offers it. A privacy mode that blanks the Mac's own screen while the phone is in use is a convenience that is left for later, as it is there.

**What is captured: one window, at the size the viewer can show.** Capture is ScreenCaptureKit with a filter for the single window that the picker returned (`init(desktopIndependentWindow:)`). Such a filter is independent of the display and the Space, always includes the whole content of the window even when it is covered, partly covered or off screen, and pauses while the window is minimised (documented, WWDC22 session 10155). No child, pop-up or other windows of the application are included, so a menu, a sheet or a popover is not in the picture (documented, same session). The property `includeChildWindows` (macOS 14.2) has no description in the reference, so what it adds and whether it is on by default are unknown (R2). Baton sets it to false, so that the picture is the window and nothing in front of it, which is also the extent of what the phone may touch (below). Frames are asked for as NV12 (`420v`, a supported pixel format, documented), which the hardware encoder takes without a conversion. The viewer's largest picture decides the size, and ScreenCaptureKit scales the window into the configured output size and says in the metadata of each frame where the content is (documented, same session), so a window of 3200 by 2000 pixels is not sent to a phone whose screen is about 2900 by 1300. The size is never more than the window's own or 3840 by 2160. When the shape of the window changes, the source reads the content rectangle from the metadata and reconfigures the stream to a new size (`updateConfiguration`, documented), and every change of the output size starts a new configuration epoch with a key frame. The units of that rectangle, points or pixels, are not in the reference (R2). The source believes it as pixels only where it is the window scaled to fit the picture, to two pixels of rounding and with a pixel of slack at the edges, since a rectangle in points would put a tap at half of where the person pointed; where it is not, a window that has the shape of the picture is taken to fill it, which is what scaling it to fit does, and any other has no geometry, so that a tap on it is dropped. The source logs which of the three it found, with the numbers of the rectangles and nothing else: no title and no text.

**A whole display can be shared too.** The same picker offers a window or a display (`allowedPickerModes` with `singleWindow` and `singleDisplay`, documented). It is presented with `present()` and not with `present(using:)`, which is for a single type of selection (documented), and the filter it returns says which (`style` is `window` or `display`, documented). The offer says it with the kind `Display` (protocol.md section 9). A display has no application, so that is empty, and its title is its name if the source knows one; the Mac source sends none in this version. Its size is the content rectangle of the filter, which is in points, times the scale from points to pixels (both documented), so a Retina display is offered at the size of its pixels (inferred, R13 checks it), and the picture is scaled down to what the viewer takes, as it is for a window. Capture is the same stream, with the filter that the picker returned. A filter for a display excludes only the windows it is told to, so everything on the display is in the picture: the menu bar, the Dock and the windows of other apps (inferred). That is what the person chose in the picker. `ignoreShadowsSingleWindow` applies in the window style only (documented). The pauses are those of a window where they apply, the Mac locked or its display off; a window that is minimised or off screen has no counterpart for a display. What happens when the display goes away (unplugged, or the lid closed with no other display) is not documented, and R13 checks it.

**Control of a display is the same choice at the Mac, and it gives the viewer the whole screen.** The person who chooses "view and control" and then picks a display lets the phone act as the person on all of it: the pointer reaches whatever the person at the Mac would reach, sheets and system dialogs included, and the keys include those that the system keeps for itself (Spotlight, the application switcher, Mission Control, force quit). Locking the screen is not blocked either, and the session then pauses until the person unlocks. That is the decision, and the consent stays at the Mac, each time: the offer names one display, carries control only if the person chose it for this offer, and the Mac shows who controls it and has a Stop button, as it does for a window (protocol.md section 9). The person in control of a display can cover or hide that card and press that button, so what stays is the yes at the Mac for this offer, the indicator that the system shows for a screen that is shared (which has a Stop button too), and the lock, which stops all input. The rules for input below are those of a window with the display in its place. A position is a place on the display, in the points of the screen of the Mac; the point being outside it, the display having another size than in the frame the event names, and the display being gone (unplugged) are the rules of a window. Everything on a display is what the viewer sees, so no window lies in front of it, and the rule about the windows in front finds none. The keyboard is the system's, so the rules about a key (the focused window, a window of the same application in front, an open menu) are not applied, and a key goes to whatever has it. No application is brought forward, since none was shared. A display is paused for the lock only, because it is never minimised. Its events are posted to the system (`CGEventPost` to the HID event tap, from a source that has the state of the hardware and does not suppress the local mouse and keyboard after a post), and the pointer of the person at the Mac moves with them. What the viewer pressed is released to the system in the same way. A first try on a Mac showed that the sandboxed app may post to the HID event tap once the person has let it (R6): clicks acted on a shared screen, and text typed on the phone reached it. The events of a window go the same way (the rule for keys below), and the log of the source says whether the pointer followed the first event. A modifier travels as a flag on the keys that follow it, so a switcher that is held open by a modifier may not stay open (inferred, R6 checks it). The arrows carry the function flag and the flag of the numeric keypad, and the function keys, Help, Home, End, Page Up, Page Down and forward delete the function flag, as a Mac keyboard sets them, because the system keeps its shortcuts for these keys (Mission Control on Control with the up arrow, the spaces on Control with the left and right arrows) with those flags (inferred, R6 checks it). A scroll is sent by the system to what lies under the pointer, and not under the place the event names (inferred, R6 checks it), so the pointer is moved to the place of every scroll first (where it is cannot be known: the person at the Mac has a pointer of their own, and a post can fail).

A screen that has not changed produces no new frame (status `idle`, documented), so a still screen costs almost nothing. The same fact is a trap: when a frame is abandoned, or the viewer asks for a key frame, while the screen is still, nothing new arrives to encode, so the source keeps the last picture and encodes it again.

**Encoding is hardware H.264 in low-latency mode, and recovery uses references that the viewer is known to hold.** The encoder is a `VTCompressionSession` created with the low-latency rate control specification (macOS 11.3 and later). That mode makes a run of P frames after the first key frame, with no frame reordering and no look-ahead, and builds a temporal layer structure (documented in the reference for the specification). The reference says that only High profiles are used and that the encoder sets the levels, and the session that introduced the mode adds Constrained Baseline and Constrained High and sets one or the other with the profile level property (documented, WWDC21 session 10158). The two do not quite agree, so R3 records which profile a Mac gives. It always uses the hardware encoder, adapts quickly to the target bitrate, and was introduced for H.264 (documented, WWDC21 session 10158). Key frames are made at the start, on a change of size and when asked, never on a timer. The quantiser is capped so that text stays legible when the rate is low (`MaxAllowedFrameQP`, documented), and the average bitrate is the one that the rule for the rate below chooses. To recover from an abandoned frame without a key frame, the encoder marks some frames as long-term references and the source tells it which of them the viewer holds, so that the next frame refers to one of those (`EnableLTR`, the acknowledgement token and `ForceLTRRefresh`, documented in the same session). The application has to supply that acknowledgement, and here it comes from the transport, below. Whether the hardware encoder of the Macs that matter does this, and how much smaller such a frame is than a key frame for a screen of text, is test R3. HEVC in low-latency mode is reported for newer systems, and R3 decides whether it is worth adding; H.264 comes first because every device decodes it and its low-latency mode is the documented one. On the wire it is H.264 in Annex B form, with the parameter sets in front of every key frame, so that a viewer that missed the configuration can start at the next key frame and a decoder on another platform can read it.

**Video travels as one unidirectional stream for each frame, and a frame that is late is reset.** After a yes the source opens a unidirectional QUIC stream for every encoded frame, writes a small binary header and the encoded bytes, and finishes it. The header has the kind of the stream (below), the session, a frame number, the number of the frame that has to be decoded before this one, the number of the chain the frame belongs to, the capture time on the source's clock, flags (key frame, recovery frame, and whether another frame refers to this one) and the epoch. A chain is a run of frames that is decoded one after the other, beginning at a key frame or at a recovery frame. Three rules keep the delay bounded:

1. **Drop before the queue.** A frame is in flight from the moment it is written until its stream has been acknowledged (`SendStream::stopped` yields `None` once the peer has acknowledged all of a finished stream, noq 1.3.0, documented there). The source encodes the next picture only if the bytes in flight are below the congestion window of the connection (iroh 1.3.0 lists the paths of a connection with the selected one marked, and the statistics of a path give its congestion window and round trip as plain numbers, `PathStats::cwnd` and `rtt`; `Connection::congestion_state` takes a path id too and is documented as being for debugging), or if fewer than two frames are in flight, and never with more than six in flight, nor while a frame that starts a chain is in flight (rule 2). A frame that starts a chain is counted after the resets it makes (rule 2), so neither the window nor the six frames hold it back; only another frame that starts a chain does, unless it is a key frame that the viewer asked for. ScreenCaptureKit sends a frame for each update of the window, at most sixty a second, from a pool of surfaces whose depth is three by default, and an app that holds on to surfaces falls behind and has to drop frames itself (documented, WWDC22 session 10155). So the source keeps the newest picture and lets go of the others at once, and what is skipped is the old. What ScreenCaptureKit does when the pool is empty is for R2. A count of frames alone would be wrong twice: a path with a long round trip needs more frames in flight to be full, and a receiver may hold back an acknowledgement for up to its maximum acknowledgement delay, 25 ms unless it asks otherwise, so a run of small frames would be held back for no reason. The window of a connection that has been idle is small, so the first key frame of a session may take several round trips; R5 reports the time to the first picture. The harness below compares this rule with fixed counts.
2. **Abandon what is late, except what starts a chain.** A frame is given a deadline when it is written: 100 ms plus two round trips plus the time that the bytes already in flight and the frame itself need to cross the path at the rate the path delivers, which is the estimate that the rule for the rate below uses (BBRv3's pacing rate, or under Cubic the window over the round trip), and never more than two seconds. A frame that has not been acknowledged by then is abandoned. The deadline is fixed when the frame is written, because one worked out again from the round trip as the queue drains would shrink while the frame waits, and it includes the time to cross the path, because without it every large frame on a slow link would be abandoned before it could arrive (150 KB takes over half a second at 2 Mbit/s). Its stream is reset (`SendStream::reset` drops what is buffered and stops retransmitting what was sent, noq 1.3.0), and so are the streams of the later frames of its chain that are still in flight. The next picture is the first frame of a new chain. It is a recovery frame that refers to a long-term reference the viewer holds, and names in its header the newest frame that the viewer is known to hold and that other frames refer to (a flag in the header) as the one to decode first, or a key frame if there is none. A frame that starts a chain makes every earlier frame that is not held useless, and those still in flight are reset. A key frame also makes the decoder forget every reference, so the source forgets the ones it held. A frame that starts a chain is never abandoned for being late, and nothing is written behind it until the viewer has acknowledged it. What would replace it is a frame of the same kind and, for a key frame, no smaller, because the cap on the quantiser keeps a key frame of a window of text from shrinking with the rate. A key frame of more than about 230 KB cannot cross a link of 1 Mbit/s, the floor of the rate below, inside the cap of two seconds, and a Retina window of text may need that much (R3 measures it). With a deadline each such frame would be reset in its turn, the bytes that had crossed would be thrown away every time, and no picture would ever arrive. A frame written behind a chain starter would fail in another way: its deadline includes the bytes of the starter and is capped too, and when it ran out it would start a new chain and reset a starter that the viewer does not hold yet. So on such a link the first picture comes late and then keeps up. While a chain starter is in flight the source makes no new frame, and the newest picture is kept as before. The one frame it does make is a key frame that the viewer asked for, which goes at the next picture and resets the starter, because a viewer that has asked cannot use what is on its way (the rule for requests is below). The rule for liveness below still ends a session whose path is gone, and the deadline applies to the frames after the starter once the viewer holds it. A frame that no other frame refers to is abandoned alone, and the chain goes on. The temporal layers of the low-latency mode make such frames when `BaseLayerFrameRateFraction` asks for them, and the sample attachment `IsDependedOnByOthers` says which frames they are (documented, WWDC21 session 10158). So in a bad moment the source can halve the frame rate without a recovery frame.
3. **Show the newest, at once.** The viewer decodes the frames of a chain in order, as soon as it has them, and shows the newest. There is no playout buffer, because a screen needs a low delay more than an even rhythm.

The source knows what the viewer holds without asking it. The only frames that do not arrive are the ones the source chose to reset, and QUIC acknowledges every other stream. A frame counts as held when it has been acknowledged and the frame it names as the one to decode first is held, which follows the names back to a key frame, because a frame that arrived early cannot be decoded without the ones it depends on. The names decide, and not the order of the numbers: a frame that no other frame refers to and that the source abandoned alone is never acknowledged, and the frames after it are held all the same. That is why a long-term reference needs no message of its own, and why a key frame is asked for only when the viewer cannot decode, which is a message on the control stream. Frames reach the viewer on separate streams and so in any order. The header of each says which frame has to have been decoded before it, and the viewer decodes a frame as soon as that one has been decoded, so it neither waits for a frame that may have been reset nor needs to know which were. A recovery frame names the newest frame that the viewer is known to hold and that other frames refer to (the flag in the header), and the viewer decodes the frames up to it first, because the recovery frame may refer to something in them that the decoder has to have. A frame that nothing refers to is not named, although the source counts it as held: the viewer may have dropped it under the next rule, because a frame with a higher number was decoded before it arrived, and a recovery frame that named it could not be decoded. The viewer decodes in increasing frame number and never goes back. A frame numbered below one that has been decoded is dropped when it arrives, and a frame that is waiting is dropped when a higher one is decoded. A reset stream can arrive whole after the recovery frame that replaced it, and decoding it then would change what the frames after it are decoded against: a decoder takes the frames in the order in which they were encoded, and a reference frame that comes late leaves an error at the next frame or, worse, a wrong picture with no error (inferred). A frame that arrives late is not a reason to ask for a key frame. The source never refers across a key frame, so a frame that depends on something from before the newest key frame was made before it and arrived late, and the reset frames that arrive whole are such frames; they are dropped. The viewer asks for a key frame in two cases only. One is a decode error. The other is a disagreement with the source: a frame above the newest one the viewer decoded names a frame below that one that the viewer did not decode, so the source counted as held a frame that the viewer does not have. Without that request the picture would stop for good, because a key frame never comes on a timer. A request names the newest frame that the viewer has handed to its decoder, whether the decoder took it or not. The source answers it with a key frame at the next picture, unless a key frame numbered above the named frame is on its way or held: the viewer has not tried that one yet, and it answers the request. So the answer does not depend on which of the request and the acknowledgement of a frame the source reads first, and they travel on different streams. A viewer whose decoder rejects the key frame itself names that key frame, which cannot answer its own request, and the next key frame follows; a rule that only asked whether a key frame was on its way would count the rejected one, and the picture would stay frozen. A frame that starts a chain and is still in flight is reset when that key frame is written, a recovery frame included: a viewer that cannot decode takes nothing but a key frame, so the recovery frame is of no use to it, and waiting for its acknowledgement would only delay the key frame. A key frame that the source makes on request is made no sooner than 250 ms after the previous key frame, so that a viewer cannot make the source encode as fast as it can ask. A request that comes sooner waits for its time and is not lost. A request that names a frame that was never sent is a malformed message and ends the session. While it waits for a key frame the viewer asks again every half second and names the same frame, which the key frame on its way answers; the source answers every request itself, so correctness does not rest on the repeat, which is a safeguard. While the viewer waits for a key frame, the frames that arrive are kept and not dropped: streams are read in any order, so a frame that the source wrote behind the key frame, and counts as held, can be read before the key frame is. The frames from before the key frame go when it has been decoded. The numbers in these rules (six frames, 100 ms) are starting values that the tests settle.

Held means acknowledged, not decoded, and two things can make the viewer hold something other than what the source counts. A reset stream can still arrive whole: noq documents that if an attempt has already been made to finish the stream, the peer may still receive all written data (`SendStream::reset`, 1.3.0), and every frame stream is finished at once. And the decoder can reject a frame. So the viewer decodes every frame it receives that it can decode, in the order above, a frame that the source has since given up on included unless a frame with a higher number has been decoded already, and on any decode error it drops the frames it is holding back and asks for a key frame. This is sound only if a frame that the viewer decodes and the source does not count cannot push out the long-term reference that the next recovery frame refers to. Whether the encoder's choice of long-term references guarantees that is unknown, and R3 and R8 test it with frames that arrive after their deadline.

**Control travels on two small streams that stay open.** After the viewer has accepted, the source opens one unidirectional stream of kind `control` to the viewer and the viewer opens one to the source. They carry length-prefixed CBOR messages in the framing of ADR 0005, with a limit of 64 KiB for a message. From the source come the configuration of the picture (codec, size, scale, rate, epoch), pause and resume with the reason (the Mac is locked, the window is gone), and notices. From the viewer come a report a few times a second (the newest frame decoded and shown, the time to decode, the frames it dropped, the thermal state of the device), what it wants (the largest picture and the highest rate, which change when the phone is turned), a request for a key frame, and, when control is granted, input. These messages are not requests. They do not count against the rate limit on items, the 16 request streams or the timer for a request, and a session has a rule of its own for liveness: the viewer reports at least every two seconds, the source stops encoding after ten seconds without a report and ends the session after thirty. A session lives and dies with its connection. If the connection closes, the session ends and the Mac's person offers again; resuming is not part of this version. Input goes on a stream and not as a request for each event because a pointer moves a hundred times a second, and a request is a stream, a frame and an answer.

**Unidirectional streams have kinds, and the budget is the sum of what each kind needs.** The first byte of every unidirectional stream says what it is: 1 for a lane of a file transfer (ADR 0008), 2 for a frame of video, 3 for a control stream. A lane begins with this byte, in front of the id of its transfer. A stream of a kind that is unknown, or that this peer may not use, is a violation: it is stopped (`STOP_SENDING`) with code 400 and not read. A stream that names a session that is not running is stopped with code 499, the code for something stopped on purpose, because a frame can arrive after its session has ended, and that is not a fault. A second stream of a kind that a session has only one of, such as control, is a violation. The gate raises the limit of unidirectional streams from zero at the same moment and by the same hook as ADR 0008, to the sum of what the kinds need at that receiver, and the limit is the receiver's, so each side counts the streams that the other opens. The viewer allows the source thirteen for streaming: twelve for frames, which are six in flight and up to six more that have been reset and are still counted by the viewer (after a stall the source resets up to six frames and opens six new ones at once), and one control stream. The source allows the viewer one, for control. Each adds eight for lanes (ADR 0008). QUIC has one limit for all unidirectional streams and does not know the kinds, so a receiver also counts the open streams of each kind, and a stream beyond the count of its kind would take the place of another kind. For a lane or a control stream that is a violation, and a violation by a stream of a session ends the session. For a frame stream it is not. The source cannot see when the viewer stops counting a reset stream (QUIC acknowledges a reset before the viewer's code has let go of the stream, and the credit for new streams is one number for every kind), so a thirteenth frame stream can reach a viewer that still counts twelve, most likely on a lossy link where frames are reset, and no one is at fault. It is stopped with code 499 and not read, like a frame of a session that is not running, and the source, which sees the stop, treats the frame as lost: the chain breaks, and a recovery frame or a key frame follows. The senders keep the same counts and open no new frame stream while twelve are open or being reset. The viewer also caps the bytes of pictures that it holds, whether a stream is still delivering them or they wait to be decoded, at the connection window (16 MiB). A picture has to be whole before it is decoded, so the viewer reads it into memory of its own, and bytes it has read no longer count against the connection window, which is otherwise the bound on what a paired device can make a device hold. With twelve frame streams of 8 MiB each a source could make the viewer hold 96 MiB, and the frames that wait for a key frame have no bound at all, because a finished stream gives its place to the next. A frame stream that would take the pictures held beyond the cap is stopped with code 499 and not read any further, like the thirteenth, and the source treats the frame as lost. An honest source stays far below the cap, since it writes nothing behind a chain starter until the viewer has acknowledged it, but the bound does not rest on the source. That holds when a source repeats a frame number too: a number that the viewer holds, or has decoded since the last key frame, is a violation that ends the session (protocol.md section 9), because the viewer names a frame by its number and could not tell two pictures with one number apart.

**Streaming comes first in the queue.** Priorities are set with `SendStream::set_priority` (higher is sent first, and the library warns that many levels cost speed, so there are three). Requests and control streams keep the default level, which is what request streams have now. Frames of video get one level below it, and lanes of a file transfer two levels below. So a ping, a notice or an input event never waits in the sender's queue behind a frame or a lane, and a frame never waits behind a lane. Credit for flow control is still first come first served, as ADR 0008 says: what a lane has written holds credit until the receiver reads it, and a frame waits for credit that lanes are holding. One lane can hold at most the window of one stream, 8 MiB of the 16 MiB connection window (ADR 0007), which leaves the other half to frames, and two lanes can hold all of it. So while a session runs, all the file transfers between the two devices share one lane in total, including a transfer that began before the session: it ends its other lanes and goes on with one, and a second transfer waits for the first. The rule can be loosened when R9 shows what a transfer costs a session. The congestion controller is one choice for the whole endpoint, which ADR 0007 and ADR 0008 both leave to measurement. BBRv3 (`Bbr3Config`, noq-proto 1.3.0) takes the delay of the path into account and Cubic does not, so R9 adds the delay of a frame during a file transfer to what the benchmark of ADR 0008 compares. Nothing here changes the controller before the two have been compared.

**The rate follows what the connection says and what the viewer reports, falls fast and rises slowly.** The source chooses the bitrate, with a floor of 1 Mbit/s and a cap of 40 Mbit/s as starting values, from three things. One is the controller's own estimate (BBRv3 reports a pacing rate and Cubic only a window, so under Cubic the estimate is the window over the round trip). One is the smoothed round trip and how fast it grows. One is the viewer's reports: the frames it dropped, and the gap between the newest frame sent and the newest shown. It lowers the frame rate or the size when the viewer reports a high thermal state. All of this is local. The wire carries reports and wishes and no formula, so the rule can change without a new protocol, and the tests compare settings.

**Input is confined to the window that was shared.** With control granted, the viewer sends events in batches of at most 64 and at most 240 events a second, and it joins the moves of a pointer into the latest one while they wait for room, which is when the rate leaves none or the stream to the source is full (a path that is only slow holds the batches in the transport, where they are not joined, and the rules below drop a press whose picture went out of date). The viewer counts the rate by its own clock and the source by the time that events arrive, which a stall on the way bunches, so the source drops what is over the rate and one batch (304 events in a second), with a notice to the viewer, and a viewer that keeps to its rate loses nothing to a stall of a quarter of a second; the session goes on, because a viewer that sends too fast has lost events and has not broken the format. A pointer position is a pair of numbers from 0 to 1 in the picture the viewer saw, with the frame number of that picture and the modifiers that are down. A finger or a pointer that leaves the picture for the bars around it would give a number outside that range, which ends the session, so the viewer clamps the position to the picture while a button is down and sends nothing outside it otherwise; the third rule below moves a position at the edge to the edge of the window. A key is a USB HID usage of the keyboard page with its modifiers and, optionally, the text it produced, which the Mac attaches to the event (`CGEventKeyboardSetUnicodeString`). The code is a usage and not a Mac virtual key code because that is what the viewer has (`UIKey.keyCode` on iPadOS, and Flutter's `PhysicalKeyboardKey.usbHidUsage`, which is reported to carry the page above the usage) and it belongs to no operating system: each source translates from one published table to its own codes, and a source that is not a Mac does not translate from the Mac's. A usage above 0xE7 is refused. A viewer sends the low 16 bits of a usage only when its page is the keyboard page, since the bits of a usage of another page (a media key) mean another key on this one. Code 0 means no key, and an event with it carries the text that the on-screen keyboard and dictation give, which have no key; the source types the text, and the event goes down once and is neither held, repeated nor released. Text is at most 20 UTF-16 units, which is as much as one call of that function is reported to keep, so that text which passes the check is text that one event carries. A viewer splits a longer text (dictation, a paste, a long sequence of emoji) at character boundaries across several events. The source keeps the geometry of every frame it sent, and maps a position onto the window using the geometry of the frame the event names. A picture is out of date from the moment the window changes, and not from the moment it was captured: the source notes the capture time of every picture that shows a change, whether it sent it or dropped it before the queue, and the first change after a frame is when the picture of that frame went out of date. A window that does not change delivers no frame (above) and so has a current picture however long ago it was captured, which is why a tap on a still window works. A frame that encodes the same picture again belongs to that picture and does not replace it. The geometry of a frame is kept until five seconds after its picture went out of date, which is longer than a picture may be out of date (below), so that a picture that is too old is told as that and not as a frame that the source does not know. Three rules decide what is delivered. The first two are about events that start something (a button or a key going down, a modifier changing, a move or a scroll) and the third about what ends it.

- **A press is dropped** when control is off, the session is paused, the Mac is locked or its display is off, the window is not on screen, the frame it names is not one the source kept, or its picture went out of date more than 2 s ago by the source's clock (a click aimed at content that has scrolled since, delivered late by a stream that was backed up; the time since the window changed counts, and not the age of the frame; 2 s is a starting value for R7. It was 500 ms until the first try on a Mac and an iPad, which dropped taps with the sentence about an old picture. A picture is out of date from the moment the window changes, and the viewer goes on showing it until the next one has reached it, so a tap on what the person sees is aged by the way of that next picture to the viewer, by the way of the event back, and by a Mac that is busy or a thread that has events waiting; the time the person took to decide is not in it, because they decided while the picture was current. So 500 ms left no room for a slow path or a busy Mac. A frame that the source does not know, because the layout of the window in its picture could not be worked out or the frame is not kept any more, is another reason and has a sentence of its own, so that the person and the log can tell the two apart), the window has changed its size since that frame (the same position in the picture would be another spot in the window; a window that only moved is no reason, because the source adds the position of the window as it is when the event arrives), the point is outside the window, or another window is in front of the window at that point (`CGWindowListCopyWindowInfo` with `optionOnScreenAboveWindow` lists the windows in front of it, documented). A sheet, a menu or a popover of the same application is such a window, so the phone cannot press anything on one (see below). A key names no frame and has no point, so the conditions about the frame, the size of the window, the point and what lies in front of it leave it alone.
- **A key goes only to the shared window.** An event that is handed to the system goes to the application in front, and the application hands it to its key window (inferred), which need not be the window that was shared: with two windows of one application, one of them shared, the phone would type into the other. So a key is delivered only while the application is in front and the shared window is its focused window. The focused-window attribute of the Accessibility API would say which it is, but a sandboxed app is reported not to be let to ask it about the windows of other applications, and no documented call turns an accessibility window into a window number (the function that does it is private, reported). So the Mac source does not use it, and judges from what the window server lists, which needs no consent: the application of the shared window is the frontmost one (documented), no window of that application on a layer that can hold the focus lies in front of the shared one, whether or not it overlaps it, and no menu of another application is open. It is a judgement, and it errs on the side of not typing: a Mac that cannot say who is in front counts as not having the keyboard, and a window server that cannot be asked fails the call, which drops the events of that batch. What it cannot see is a window of the application that has the keyboard while it lies behind the shared one (a sheet of another window of the application, or a window that the application makes key without ordering it forward), and R6 checks how often that happens with ordinary applications. A key is also dropped while a window of the same application lies in front of the shared window and overlaps it, or is an open menu wherever it is on the screen. A menu takes the keys while the shared window stays the focused window of the application (inferred), and the phone can open one itself with a tap on a pop-up button or a right click, so without this condition the arrows, a letter and Return would choose from a menu that the phone cannot see; a sheet may behave in the same way. A menu can also open away from the shared window: the Help menu opens under the menu bar, and the application handles the shortcut that opens it itself (inferred), so a key from the phone can open it, and the keys after that would type into its search field and run what it finds. So the layer of an open menu, which R6 records, counts without the overlap, and every other layer keeps it. The query of the first rule lists the windows in front, and the process that owns each (`kCGWindowOwnerPID`), its layer (`kCGWindowLayer`) and its bounds (`kCGWindowBounds`) are documented keys of what it returns. The reference gives the layer as a number and does not say which a menu, a sheet or a tooltip is on, so R6 says which layers count, so that a tooltip or a floating panel does not stop the typing. A floating panel still covers what lies under it for a press, so if R6 finds layers that cover a press and take no keys, each window in the list gets a flag for whether it can take keys, and the rules stay as they are. The phone is not stuck behind a menu: a tap on the window outside the menu is not dropped by the rule for a press, because nothing lies in front of the window at that point, and it closes the menu. The source brings the application of the window to the front once, at the first event that comes for it while the window is on the screen, and waits for it to come for a second at the most in all, since the events that wait behind that wait are judged by the time that they are put to the window (the Mac is asked about the window before each batch of events; a locked Mac is not touched, and neither is a window that is minimized, hidden, closed or on another desktop, since bringing its application forward would show it again or switch the desktop of the person at the Mac), and does not take the focus back after that by itself (a tap on the window does, since the events go to the system and a tap brings the application forward as a click of the person at the Mac does: inferred). Shortcuts that act on the system and not on the window (Spotlight, the app switcher, Mission Control, force quit, locking the screen, and the keyboard navigation of the system, Control with F1 to F8, which takes the keys to the menu bar, the Dock, another window or the menus of the status items, where the Apple menu offers Lock Screen and Shut Down) are dropped, by a list that the Mac source keeps (`isSystemShortcut` in `BatonInput.swift`). A key matches the list when it is held with at least the modifiers of an entry, so the list errs on the side of dropping. The events of a window go to the system, as those of a display do, and the system acts on these shortcuts before the application sees them. Posting the events to the window's own process (`CGEventPostToPid`) was tried first, in the hope that it would keep the global shortcuts from seeing them, which would have been better than a list. On a Mac, from an iPad and from an iPhone, a click posted so showed a tap on the phone and did not act in the window, where the same click handed to the system acted on a screen (R6). Why is inferred: such an event does not move the pointer, and a button that is pressed looks at where the pointer is and not at where the event says. So the pointer of the person at the Mac moves with the phone's, and the list is what keeps the shortcuts out. It holds the defaults of the system and of the few things that it keeps for itself (the switcher, force quit, logging out), so a Mac whose shortcuts were changed may act on other keys. A key that the list leaves out is not posted, its release is not either, and the viewer is told as for a rule that this version does not know. A key whose press went through is released when it is let go of, even if its repeats come with the modifiers of a shortcut by then (a modifier goes down while the key is held): only a press that did not go through is left out with its release. On a whole display none of this is judged and the shortcuts act (control of a display, above).
- **A press that was delivered always gets its release.** The source remembers every button, key and modifier it has pressed, and delivers the release of each whatever the other rules say, so that a drag that leaves the window, a window that appears during a press, a window that loses the keyboard in the middle of one, or control turned off cannot leave a button or a key held down in the application. While a button is down, a position outside the window is moved to the window's edge and delivered, so a drag ends where it left, and positions are put on the screen relative to the position of the window at the press, so that a window dragged by its title bar follows the finger and does not run ahead of it (R7 checks this). A release of something that this session did not press is dropped. When the source stops delivering (control turned off, a pause, a lock, the end of the session, or ten seconds without a report from the viewer) it first releases everything it has pressed, to the system.

Whenever a rule drops an event, the viewer is told which rule it was, at most once a second, so that a tap that did nothing has a reason that the person can read. The message holds no title and no text that was typed, and neither does the log.

**The phone says what the person did, and the Mac decides what happens.** A window that was shared with control has a Control switch in the bar of the screen that shows it, and nothing is sent until the person turns it on. While it is off, two fingers zoom the picture, as they do for a window that is only looked at. While it is on, the picture stays as it is and the fingers are the window's, as the events of a mouse. A finger that lifts after moving no more than 10 points is a click: a move to the place, then a press and a release of the left button. A finger that stays for 500 ms is a click of the right button at the place where it touched, and the phone taps, because a right click shows the person nothing on the phone. A finger that moves further than 10 points presses the left button at the place where it first touched and follows the finger to where it lifts, so that the slop does not move the start of a drag. Two fingers that move scroll the window at the middle between them, by the distance that the middle moved times a gain, so that the content follows the fingers. A third finger, and whatever follows a gesture that the system took away or that became another kind, is left alone until every finger is up, and a drag that a second finger turns into a scroll lets go of the button first. Every position is clamped to the picture, so a drag that leaves it ends at its edge. The picture is drawn smaller than the window, and the Mac scales a Retina window to what the phone takes, so the gain is the pixels of the picture over its points on the screen, over two, and at least 1 and at most 8. It, the 10 points and the 500 ms are starting values, and R7 sets them. The modifiers of an event are those of a keyboard that is attached to the phone and those that the person chose on the bar of the screen (Shift, Ctrl, Option and Cmd, the last being a key that a phone has no other way to press). What the person chose is used up by the next thing that is done, so that a Cmd that was chosen for one key does not stay on for the next.

A key of a keyboard that is attached to the phone goes as a key event with its usage of the keyboard page (`PhysicalKeyboardKey.usbHidUsage`, taken only when its page is the keyboard page), the modifiers that are down, whether it repeats, and the text it made; the event for a key going up never has text. A key with no usage on that page (the Globe key, a media key) is left to the system, and the modifier keys are no events of their own, because the modifiers come with every event. The phone keeps the keys that it sent as down, so that a key which went down before control was turned on, and comes up after, sends nothing; turning control off, leaving the screen and the window ending let go of every key and button that was sent, as the source does (above). The keys are taken by the focus scope of the screen and not by a handler of the keyboard that sees every key, because the focus tree gives a key to every widget between the focus and the root until one takes it: the shortcuts of the app (Tab moves the focus, Return and Space press the button that has it) and the field of the keyboard of the screen (Cmd with Backspace deletes up to the start of the line on an Apple device) would act on a key that the window had already taken, and a key would also reach the window while a dialog lies over the screen. A key that the window takes stops in the scope; a key that it does not take, like the Globe key, and every key while control is off, goes on to the app. The keyboard of the screen makes no key events, so the phone gives it a field that cannot be seen and that keeps no text. The field holds 32 zero-width spaces, because a Backspace deletes nothing from an empty field and tells nobody, and with something to delete it is seen. The phone reads each change of the field as a number of characters deleted and the text typed after them, and makes the field whole again. The deletions go as Backspace keys and the text as text with code 0, with a line end as the Return key and a tab as the Tab key, because a field of the Mac does not take them as text. While Ctrl, Option or Cmd is down, a character that a key of a US keyboard makes goes as that key, because a shortcut is made of keys and not of letters. Text that is still being composed, as it is for Chinese or Japanese or while the keyboard guesses a word, goes when it is done. Below the picture a bar has what the keyboard of a phone does not: the four modifier keys, Esc, Tab, the arrows, and the button that brings the keyboard up and puts it down. What the Mac dropped comes back as a sentence over the picture for four seconds, and it lets the touches through.

The Mac offers a window to be controlled from the second entry of the menu of the share button of a device. It asks the system whether Baton may post events when the person chooses the entry, and not at the first event: if the person has not said yes, it asks the system to ask, opens the list in System Settings where the person says yes (the question of the system is shown once, and the list is hard to find by hand), says in a sentence where to say yes and shares nothing until the person chooses again. Before macOS 15.2 the system does not say which window the person picked, so a window cannot be steered and the button offers only the view.

A sheet, a menu or a popover of the shared application is not part of the shared window in this version. The capture leaves it out (above), it lies in front of the window and so covers it, and a key meant for it is dropped by the second rule, so the phone shows a window that does not answer and says that the Mac needs its person. Showing these windows and letting the phone use them needs a reliable way to tell the child windows of the shared window from other windows of the same application, and it is a later change once R2 and R6 have shown one.

Posting events needs the person's consent, asked for the first time a session with control starts (`CGRequestPostEventAccess`, macOS 10.15 and later, documented; the Accessibility pane of System Settings, reported). Whether a sandboxed app can post events at all is not established (R6). The Mac app is sandboxed now and is installed from Xcode, so the sandbox is not required for distribution. If R6 says that it cannot, the choices are to give up the sandbox for the Mac app or to move the posting into a small helper that has no other job and is signed with the same identity. That changes what the Mac app is allowed to do, so it is put to the project's owner when R6 has a result and is not decided here. Everything a viewer sends is checked before it is used: numbers must be finite and in range, counts are bounded, and a malformed message ends the session.

**Where the code lives: safe Rust where it can be tested anywhere, Swift where it touches Apple's frameworks.** `baton-stream` is a new crate with no platform code and no input or output: the types of the control messages and of the frame header, the accounting of frames in flight and of chains, the rule for the rate, the viewer's reordering, and the checks on input as pure functions. It is tested with a simulated link. The session (the two kinds of request, opening and accepting streams, kinds, priorities, the budget) is in `baton-core` next to the gate and the lanes. The Apple side is a Flutter plugin in the repository with Swift code for macOS and iOS: the picker and capture, the encoder, the decoder, the texture that Flutter shows, the permissions, and the posting of input. The texture is a `FlutterTexture` whose `copyPixelBuffer` is called on the raster thread and may return an NV12 buffer, which is what the decoder produces (Flutter engine header). Frames do not pass through Dart. Swift and Rust exchange them through a small C interface, frames and events in both directions, and its Rust side needs `unsafe` functions. CONTRIBUTING.md allows `unsafe` in a platform crate of its own with the reason written down, so the interface is a crate, `baton-stream-abi`, whose only job is to wrap safe functions of `baton-stream` with their safety contracts and tests, and `baton-ffi` links it into the library that the app already loads. Dart starts and stops sessions and shows their state through `baton-app` and the bridge, as for everything else. A Mac can be a viewer with the same code, which gives a way to see the whole path on one Mac before a phone is involved.

**Limits.** A frame is at most 8 MiB, a control message 64 KiB, a picture 3840 by 2160 pixels, a batch of input 64 events and 240 a second. A Mac has one session at a time in this version, with one viewer. The wire format and the rest of the limits are specified in protocol.md when the types that carry them are added.

```
source (Mac)                                                   viewer (iPhone or iPad)
  | -- Offer {session, title, size, control?} ------------>      |  request stream, may wait for the person
  | <------------------------ Accepted {size, rate, codecs}      |
  | == control stream: Config {epoch, size, rate} =========>     |  unidirectional, stays open, default priority
  | <========= control stream: Report, Want, KeyFrame, Input     |  the same, in the other direction
  | -- one stream for each frame: header + H.264 ==========>     |  unidirectional, one level lower, reset when late
  | -- Stop {session} ------------------------------------->     |  request stream, either side may send it
```

## How it will be measured

The part that continuous integration can show is built first. The part that needs a Mac and a phone is a list of numbered tests, as in locked-devices.md section 8, and the pull request that builds a piece says which of them it has run.

**Without Apple hardware, on every pull request.**

- **Frame delivery on a real connection.** The emulated path of ADR 0007 (two nodes, a delay line, a rate), with loss and a bounded queue added and with `netem` on a pair of virtual network devices on Linux, carries a synthetic stream of frames sized like a screen: a key frame of 150 KB, deltas of 5 to 40 KB, sixty a second. A synthetic decoder checks order, chains and recovery, and rejects frames, key frames included, with the request for a key frame and the acknowledgement of a frame read in either order. A run reports the delay from write to delivery (median, 95th percentile and worst), the frames dropped, and the time to recover from a stall of 200 ms and from one of 2 s, and, on a link so slow that a key frame needs longer than the cap of two seconds, the time to the first picture, which must come. The runs compare the rule for frames in flight with fixed counts (1, 2, 4), the deadline (50, 100 and 200 ms plus two round trips) and Cubic against BBRv3, each with an idle connection, with a file transfer on the lanes, and with a ping loop, so that what priorities do and what a credit update costs are measured and not guessed. They settle the starting values of this record.
- **Budgets and kinds.** A stranger opens no unidirectional stream. A paired device gets the sum of the budgets. A stream of an unknown kind, of a kind that is not allowed, or for a session that is not running is stopped. A lane and a frame are told apart by the first byte.
- **Input.** The checks and the test for what lies in front of the window are pure functions, tested over their ranges: not-a-number and infinite values, huge counts, rates, a window that moved or was resized, one that is covered, a point on the edge, a picture that went out of date, a window that has been still for a minute, a window of the same application in front when a key comes, a menu of it away from the window, a release for every press that was delivered.
- **The interface between Swift and Rust.** A test written in Rust plays the part of the platform, pushes frames and events through the C interface in both directions, and checks that nothing leaks and that a wrong call is refused.
- **On a hosted Mac, which has no screen.** The Swift code builds, and its tests encode and decode synthetic pictures if the runner has a VideoToolbox encoder, which is unknown. A test that cannot run says so, so that a skip is not read as a pass.

**On a Mac and a phone.** Record the macOS and iOS builds, the models, the Wi-Fi band or cable, and whether the display was on. Report the median of at least ten runs with the worst one.

- **R1. Permission and the picker.** With Screen Recording refused for Baton, present the picker and choose a window. Does capture start, with or without a prompt? Does macOS ask again after some weeks (reported for macOS 15 and later)? Decides whether Baton asks for the permission at all and what its screen says.
- **R2. The shape of capture.** Check on a real window what WWDC22 session 10155 documents (a covered or off-screen window keeps delivering its whole content, a minimised one pauses, child and pop-up windows are not included), and what it does not say: another Space, full screen, a resize while streaming, the display off, the Mac locked, what `includeChildWindows` adds and whether it is on by default, what happens when the source holds on to its surfaces, and the units of the content rectangle of a frame, in points or pixels. Also: what a session does when its window is closed. If ScreenCaptureKit ends the stream, the capture ends the session; if it does not, the pause watch counts a window that is gone as hidden and the session stays paused until someone stops it, and ending it with a sentence once the window server has said twice in a row that the window is gone would tell the person what happened. Record the status, the size and the rate of the frames. Decides which states pause a session.
- **R3. Encode.** Low-latency H.264 and HEVC at 1080p and at the size of a Retina window, at 30 and at 60 frames a second: the time from the call to the callback, the bytes for a still screen of text, a scroll and a video, how text looks at the cap on the quantiser, the size of a key frame against that of a frame that refers to a long-term reference, the profile that the mode gives, and whether a frame that is decoded after the source gave up on it leaves the long-term reference of the next recovery frame in place (with R8). Decides the codec, the cap, and whether recovery uses references.
- **R4. Decode and show.** On the oldest iPhone or iPad that is to be supported and on the newest: the decode time, frames dropped, and the thermal state after ten minutes. Decides the largest size and rate.
- **R5. Delay from glass to glass.** A running clock in the shared window, an iPad showing the stream, and a phone that films both screens at 240 frames a second. Twenty readings of the difference between the two clocks, on a home Wi-Fi, over a cable and through the relay, and the time from the offer to the first picture. The aim is a median of at most 100 ms and a worst case of at most 250 ms on Wi-Fi. They are aims, which the test confirms or changes.
- **R6. Posting input from a sandboxed app.** With the consent given, post a click, a drag, a scroll, letters, a shortcut inside the app and a system shortcut, both to the system and to the window's process, into a window that is in front, one that is covered, and one on another Space. Also: two windows of one application with the keyboard in the one that is not shared (the key must be dropped), a sheet and a menu of the shared application (open one from the phone, with a tap on a pop-up button and with a right click, and the Help menu with its shortcut, which opens away from the shared window, and type: no key may reach any of them; and which window layers a menu, a sheet, a popover and a tooltip have, so that only the windows that take keys count), a drag that leaves the window, a button and a key held when control is turned off or the Mac locks (nothing may stay held), text typed with no key (an event with a string attached and key code 0, which is the A key: whether an application types the string or the letter of the key), 20 units of text in one event and a longer text in several, and whether the judgement from the windows that the window server lists (above) is right for ordinary applications, with the keyboard in a window that lies in front of the shared one, in one that lies behind it and in a sheet of another window of the application. Also: typing into the address bar of a browser, a search field or a code editor, which opens a list of suggestions under the field. That list is a window of the application, in front of the shared one and over it, on a layer that R6 records (inferred: that of ordinary windows), so the judgement would take it for the keyboard being elsewhere and nothing would be typed from the second letter on, although the field in the shared window still has the keyboard. R6 shows how often that happens and on which layer such a list is, so that it can be left out of the count. For a whole display the same posts go to the system (`CGEventPost` to the HID event tap), into the main display and into one that lies left of it or above it, whether the pointer of the Mac follows the first event (the log of the source says so), the shortcuts that the system keeps for itself, and the application switcher, whose modifier travels as a flag on the keys and may not hold it open. Decides the sandbox, the way of delivery, the rules for keys and releases, and the list of dropped shortcuts. Answered so far, on a MacBook (with an iPhone for the screen, and with an iPhone and an iPad for the window): the sandboxed app may post to the HID event tap once the person has let it in System Settings (Accessibility), clicks handed to the system acted on a shared screen, and text typed on the phone reached it. The same click posted to the process of a window showed a tap on the phone and did not act in the window, so a window is steered through the system as a display is, and the shortcuts are dropped by a list. Not yet checked: a scroll, a drag, a right click, the keys that carry the function flag (Control with an arrow, which opens Mission Control), the application switcher, Control with F2 and then Return (no menu may open on the Mac, since the key is left out), keys and shortcuts in a window, and the line of the log that says whether the pointer followed, for a window.
- **R7. Input in use.** Tap to visible change (also on a window that has been still for a minute), the feel of a drag and of a scroll, dragging the window itself by its title bar and by an edge, typing with the phone's keyboard and with the iPad's hardware keyboard, and the numbers that the phone starts with (the 10 points within which a finger is still a click, the 500 ms of a right click, the gain of a scroll, and whether the haptic tap at the right click is felt), and the first touch, which brings the application to the front once: through the cooperative activation of macOS 14 (`yieldActivation(to:)` and `activate(from:options:)`, documented), and, when the application is not in front 0.4 s after the request at the most, by opening it with `NSWorkspace`, which is not held to the cooperation (inferred). Whether either works from the background, while the person at the Mac is using another application, is unknown, and R7 checks it with Baton in the background. Decides the batching, the joining of moves, and how long the picture that an event names may have been out of date (500 ms at first, 2 s since the first try on hardware). The source logs how the events of a window went (how many, which rule dropped them, how many presses went to a window that did not have the keyboard, how many events were lost to a full queue, how long the picture that they named had been out of date, also for a press that went through, how long the Mac took to answer), never which events or where, and a count above five is told as "more than five", since a key is two events and a password is typed one key at a time. So a try that fails can be read from the Diagnostics screen of the Mac and of the other device.
- **R8. Loss and delay.** The Network Link Conditioner on the Mac, and on the phone through the Developer settings of a phone that Xcode has prepared (reported), with its profiles, a stall of 2 s, 5% loss and a rate low enough that a key frame needs longer than the cap of two seconds: the time to recover (and that the first picture comes on the slow link), whether the picture is ever wrong (also with frames held back until after their deadline, so that they arrive after the source gave up on them), how the bitrate follows. Decides the deadline and the rule for the rate.
- **R9. A transfer at the same time.** Stream while a file of 4 GB and thousands of small files go in both directions: the delay of frames and of input against an idle connection, with Cubic and with BBRv3. Needs file transfer. Decides priorities, the rule for transfers during a session, and the controller.
- **R10. Lock, sleep and leaving.** Lock the Mac with a session running (menu, hot corner, keyboard), let the display sleep, close the lid with and without a display attached, start the screen saver, and unlock: does capture resume, what does the phone show, and can anything reach the lock screen (it must not)? Does the power assertion keep the Mac from locking itself when it is idle? On the phone: Baton in the background, locking, Wi-Fi to cellular, Airplane mode. Decides the rules for pausing and resuming. It overlaps M8 to M10 of locked-devices.md.
- **R11. Energy and heat.** Thirty minutes on a Mac (processor, graphics, power) and on a phone (battery, thermal state). Decides the defaults for rate and size.
- **R12. Other networks.** The same on a cable, on 5 GHz and 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi, and between two networks through the relay.
- **R13. A whole display.** Does one picker, presented with `present()` and both modes allowed, offer a window and a display, and can the person choose either? Pick a display in the picker, with Screen Recording refused for Baton and with it allowed: does capture start, with or without a prompt? What the filter gives for `contentRect` and `pointPixelScale` on the built-in Retina display, on an external display, and with the display set to a scaled resolution (the size that the phone is offered must be the pixels of the display, not its points). Is the cursor in the picture, and does a still screen deliver no frames (`idle`) as a window does? What happens to the stream when the display goes away (unplugged, the lid closed, the Mac asleep) and when the person switches to another Space or runs an app in full screen? Decides the size that is offered, and the pauses and the sentences for a display. The first question is answered: on a MacBook the one picker offered a screen and the person chose it, and it was shown on a phone. The sizes, the cursor, the idle frames and what happens when a display goes away have not been checked. For control, a click has to land where the picture shows it on the built-in display, on an external one and on one that lies left of the main display (negative coordinates).

A piece that only a Mac can check is never described as tested.

## Alternatives considered

- **WebRTC.** It brings congestion control, jitter buffers and loss recovery that have been used for years, and a Flutter plugin exists. It also brings its own ICE and TURN beside the hole punching and relay that iroh already has, its own DTLS-SRTP handshake whose fingerprints would have to be tied to Baton's device keys, signalling to carry, a large native library, and no place for the gate, the budgets and the rule that a stranger gets nothing. The roadmap asked whether video should use QUIC or WebRTC. For the first version it is QUIC, and R8 and R12 can reopen it if the congestion handling turns out to be the weak part.
- **Datagrams for the frames.** Unreliable delivery fits video, but then the loss recovery, the reassembly of a frame from several packets and the pacing are Baton's to write. Datagram support is a transport parameter that is fixed in the handshake, so it cannot be raised after the trust check as streams can, and the pairing connections would be able to send datagrams too (the receive buffer is bounded and drops the oldest, so the harm is small). protocol.md section 3 says that peers do not send them. Resetting a late stream gives the same "the newer frame wins" without a recovery layer. If R8 shows that the retransmission delay on a lossy Wi-Fi hurts, this is the first thing to reconsider.
- **One long-lived stream for all the frames.** It would save a stream for each frame and give order for free. But the sender could not learn which frames were delivered, since noq has no per-stream count of acknowledged bytes and `stopped` completes only when a finished stream is fully acknowledged, and abandoning one late frame would mean resetting the stream and with it the frames behind it that were fine.
- **A second connection for media, with an ALPN of its own.** It would keep requests out of the video's credit and give video a controller of its own. It would cost one of the eight connections a device may keep, a second handshake and a second gate, and the two controllers would compete for one Wi-Fi link, while one connection lets the sender decide that video goes before bulk data. It is the next step if R9 shows that credit sharing hurts, as in ADR 0008.
- **Cropping at the source, for detail where the phone is zoomed in.** It needs a capture of the display with a filter that includes the window, because the crop rectangle is ignored when a single window is captured (documented). It is a later change, and it has to deal with what else is on that display. Until then the viewer zooms the picture it has. Drawing the cursor on the phone from its position, so that it moves without the delay of the pipeline, is a later improvement of the same kind.
- **Show the sheets, menus and popovers of the shared window, and let the phone use them.** It is what a person expects, since a Save sheet could then be answered from the phone. It needs a reliable way to tell the windows that belong to the shared window from the other windows of the same application, so that the rule for what lies in front of the window and the rule for where a press may land treat them as part of it and nothing else. The reference does not say what `includeChildWindows` adds, and it is not established how a program in another process could tell a child window from any other. It is left for when R2 and R6 have shown a way, and until then what the phone shows is what it can use.
- **Apple's frameworks from Rust, with objc2.** I checked that it is possible. The objc2 bindings (version 0.3, licensed Zlib, Apache-2.0 or MIT) cover ScreenCaptureKit, VideoToolbox, Core Media and Core Graphics, including the session, encode and decode calls, the keys for low latency, the quantiser and long-term references, and the posting of events, and a small probe that names the types this design needs type-checks for `aarch64-apple-darwin` on Linux, which would catch mistakes earlier than a macOS runner. But the session, encoder and decoder calls are all `unsafe`, so the crate would be mostly `unsafe`; the texture protocol of Flutter is declared in Objective-C headers that have no Rust bindings; and Apple's documentation and samples for these calls are in Swift. The Swift route is chosen. The Rust side keeps what can be tested anywhere.
- **Frames through Dart and platform channels, with no new `unsafe`.** Every frame would cross the UI isolate twice and be copied at each hop, and a phone that is busy drawing would add jitter. It is the fallback if the C interface is turned down, and a way to measure what the direct path saves.

## Prior art and where this differs

Hardware encoders in low-latency mode with long-term references are how conferencing applications on Apple platforms work, and Apple documents the mode for that use. Resetting a stream whose data has become stale, and prioritising streams, are what the working group's draft for Media over QUIC Transport describes for live media: a delivery timeout after which the sender resets the stream, and priorities set by the publisher and the subscriber. The draft was read while this record was written, and it is still changing. WebRTC answers the same problem with datagrams and feedback (negative acknowledgements, picture loss indications, forward error correction). Remote desktop software is a field with a long history. This record does not claim to have invented any of it.

What it adds, as far as I know, and I have not searched exhaustively:

- **The transport's acknowledgement of each frame, used as the encoder's acknowledgement of references.** Because only frames that the source itself resets can be lost, QUIC's acknowledgements say what the viewer holds, so recovery needs neither a round trip for a key frame nor a feedback message of the viewer's own.
- **A session that is part of the pairing trust model.** The budget for streams is granted after the trust check, the Mac's person decides what to share and whether to allow control, and the viewer cannot start capture on a Mac.
- **Input checked against what is on screen, not only against consent.** Remote control tools usually post input at screen coordinates once consent is given. Here an event is dropped unless its point is inside the shared window and nothing is in front of the window at that point. On a whole display the point has to be inside the display.
- **The viewer's thermal state in the report**, so that the source lowers the rate before the phone throttles. It is small, and it may exist elsewhere.

## Consequences

- The transport changes in one place: the limit of unidirectional streams is zero until the gate raises it for a paired device, to the sum of the budgets, and a test shows both halves, as it does for the window. Priorities are three fixed levels. The controller stays as it is until the benchmarks of ADR 0008 and R9 have been compared.
- protocol.md section 3 says that after pairing a peer may open unidirectional streams for transfers (section 8, where each lane begins with its kind), and it is extended when the stream format is specified: also for streams of video and control, each beginning with its kind. The rule of section 8 that the lanes a receiver grants add up to no more than the limit of unidirectional streams that it announces becomes the lanes' share of that limit, 8 of the 21 that a viewer announces.
- Requests of a new kind and capabilities that guard them are compatible within the major version (protocol.md section 5). An older device is never sent an offer it cannot read.
- A viewer can make the Mac encode at the size and rate it asks for, within the limits above, and a source can make the viewer hold at most what ADR 0007 says. The viewer decodes bytes that another device made. VideoToolbox does that in a system service, and only for a session that the person accepted from a paired device, but a decoder is a large attack surface, so the viewer decodes nothing before the accept, checks sizes before it allocates, and does not parse the video beyond the header and the boundaries of the units.
- A whole display can be shared, to be looked at or to be looked at and controlled. The Mac offers it with the kind `Display`, and with control only if the person chose it for this offer. Control of a display is control of the whole screen, system shortcuts included, which is why the consent stays at the Mac, each time.
- The Mac asks for Screen Recording the first time a person shares, and for the Accessibility consent the first time a session with control starts. Both are settings that the person controls in macOS, and Baton shows their state. Window titles are shown and never logged, and no picture is written to disk.
- The Mac stays awake and unlocked during a session, and the person is told. It does not lock itself when it is idle either, while the person is away by design, so the notice says so and the hand-over offers to lock the Mac when the session ends. This is the one place where the requirement to work while the source is locked is not met, because no documented API allows it. R10 and M8 will say whether that can change.
- The Mac app may have had to give up its sandbox, or use a helper, to post input. The first try on a Mac (R6) says it need not: with the consent, the sandboxed app posts to the HID event tap, and the events of windows and of displays go there. If a Mac or a later system turns out not to allow it, that is decided again, with the same fallback.
- The events of a window go to the system, as those of a display do. The pointer of the Mac moves with the phone's, and the person at the Mac keeps their own pointer and keyboard (nothing is suppressed locally), so the two share one pointer while control is on. What keeps the events inside the window is the gate: the point inside the window with nothing in front of it, and the keyboard in the window. The Mac source keeps the shortcuts of the system out of a window by a list. Between the check and the post, something can change, such as the person at the Mac clicking elsewhere or a window opening, and the event then goes to whatever has the pointer or the keyboard (inferred). That takes a few milliseconds for one event, and longer for an event late in a batch, which waits for the posts before it (a batch holds up to 64 events, and the Mac is asked about its windows once for the batch), and the first mouse event of a session holds the thread for up to 0.1 s while the log checks whether the pointer followed.
- A sheet, a menu or a popover of the shared application is neither shown to the phone nor usable from it in this version, and the phone says that the Mac needs its person. Showing and using them is a later change that needs R2 and R6.
- iOS 27 offers ScreenCaptureKit to iPhone and iPad apps too (documented), which is a route for the reverse direction, an iPhone or iPad showing its own screen to a Mac, view only. It is not designed here.
- There is no audio in this version, and no clipboard or file drag inside a session; handoffs and transfers go on beside it, on the same connection.
- Order of work, each step a small pull request: first the part that needs no Apple hardware (the messages, the kinds, the budget and priorities, the rules for frames and rate, and the benchmark with a synthetic stream); then the Mac side (the picker, capture and encoding, with a view on the same Mac); then the viewer on the iPhone and iPad; then input from the phone; then the one-step hand-over from the Mac, with its policies, indicators and the rules for locking. The steps that need a Mac and a phone say which of R1 to R12 they have run, and the numbers go into this record when they exist. Until then the aims are goals and not results.
